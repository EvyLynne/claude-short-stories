# The Night Gardener Wipes His Feet

It was a dark and stormy night on Miller Creek, which was the only kind the Night Gardener would work.

He stood at the end of the lane in his long black coat, rain dripping from the brim of his hat and from the blades of a pair of hedge shears as long as a man's arm. His face was a cracked porcelain doll mask with painted rosy cheeks. He had chosen it because it was terrifying, and also because it had been on sale.

His real name was Mortimer Crane. Nobody had called him that in years. Nobody had called him anything in years, which was partly the point.

He had a list.

Nobody knew how he chose the houses. The newspapers had theories. The police had a corkboard with red string on it. Mortimer had never told a soul, and he was not going to start now.

Tonight's house was at the end of the lane: small, white, with a ramp up to the porch and a television flickering blue behind the curtains.

On the roof of the old stone church across the road, Old Spout the gargoyle watched him go by.

"Wrong house, chum," said Old Spout. "Trust me. You don't want that one."

Mortimer turned up his collar against the wind and kept walking.

Thunder rolled. Lightning flashed. The Night Gardener crept up the ramp, which was the only way up, and took hold of the doorknob.

It wasn't locked.

He paused, a little hurt. Nobody had ever left it unlocked before. It took something out of the moment.

He pushed the door open, very slowly, so it would creak.

It did not creak. Somebody had oiled it.

He stepped inside.

"Wipe your feet," said a voice.

Mortimer froze.

In the living room, in a recliner the color of a mushroom soup, sat a very small, very old woman in a pink housecoat, with a crocheted afghan over her knees and a walker parked beside her. Its feet had tennis balls on them. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a large white button.

Her name was Agnes Pettibone, and she was eighty-seven years old.

She did not look at him. She was looking at the television.

The Night Gardener raised his shears. Lightning flashed through the window behind him at exactly the right moment. He had practiced this.

"Agnes Pettibone," he rasped. "Your time has—"

"Shh," said Agnes. "It's the semifinal."

Mortimer lowered the shears an inch.

"I said," he began again, louder, "your time has—"

"Young man." She still didn't look at him. "If you're going to stand there dripping, at least move. You're in front of the oven."

He looked at the screen.

On it, in a town square lit with jack-o'-lanterns, a witch in an apron was holding a pie up to a camera. A banner across the bottom read: *THE GREAT HALLOWEEN TOWN BAKE-OFF — LIVE.* At the judges' table sat a scarecrow in a tuxedo, a very large pumpkin in parade ribbons, and a small orange pumpkin wearing a silver crown.

"What is this?" said the Night Gardener.

"Channel nine, if you jiggle the antenna," said Agnes. "Comes in clear on stormy nights. That's Griselda. She's on pie twelve. The first eleven were a disgrace."

"I don't care about pie."

"Then you're no use to me. Move."

He did not move. He was the Night Gardener. He took one long, menacing step toward the recliner.

Agnes, sensing that she was no longer going to be left alone, decided to get up and deal with this herself.

She reached for the walker. She got halfway out of the chair.

Then she tipped sideways, very slowly, like a tree in no particular hurry, and went down on the rug.

"Oh, for heaven's sake," said Agnes, from the floor.

Mortimer stared at her.

He had been in a great many houses. He had never once been in a house where the victim fell down before he'd done anything.

"Well, don't just stand there," said Agnes. "Pick me up. I can't see the screen from down here."

"I'm a serial killer."

"And I'm on the floor. One problem at a time."

He hesitated. He set down his shears against the coffee table, because he needed both hands. He got her under the arms and lifted her back into the recliner. She weighed about as much as a bag of groceries.

"Afghan," said Agnes.

He tucked the afghan back over her knees.

"You missed the crust reveal," she told him. "That was the best part."

He picked up the shears again.

"Agnes Pettibone—"

"Commercial in two minutes," said Agnes. "You can say your piece then. I'm not unreasonable."

The Night Gardener stood in the middle of the living room holding giant hedge shears and waited for a commercial.

On the television, the small pumpkin with the crown took a bite of pie, chewed thoughtfully, and announced that it had a lovely bottom.

"He's very fair," said Agnes. "Last week he gave a seven to a pie that bit him."

The commercial came.

Agnes muted it, turned in her chair, and looked at him properly for the first time.

She took in the coat, the hat, the shears, and the mask with its painted rosy cheeks.

"Is that a doll?"

"It's terrifying."

"It's got dimples."

"It was on sale."

"I can tell." She squinted at him. "What do they call you?"

He drew himself up. "The papers call me the Night Gardener."

"Do you garden?"

"No."

"Then that's false advertising. My nephew got in trouble for that, selling vitamins. Why my house?"

He opened his mouth to explain.

The commercial ended.

"Shh," said Agnes, and unmuted the television.

Mortimer closed his mouth.

The first round ended with Griselda's pie on top. Agnes clapped, then reached for her tea, which was on the side table just beyond where she could comfortably reach. She leaned. The recliner leaned with her. Physics did the rest.

She went down again.

"That's twice," said Mortimer, lifting her back up.

"It's Tuesday," said Agnes. "I usually manage four. Make yourself useful. The kettle's on the stove."

"I'm not making you tea."

"Then I'll get up and make it myself," said Agnes, putting her hands on the armrests.

"No, no, no." He was already in the kitchen.

He came back with tea. He'd found the good cups on his own, behind the cereal. Agnes approved of that.

"One sugar?"

"Two. And sit down, you're making me nervous."

"I'm supposed to make you nervous."

"You're making me nervous that you'll block the screen."

He sat on the very end of the sofa, with the shears across his knees.

Outside, the storm howled. Inside, the Great Halloween Town Bake-Off went into its second round. It was the showstopper, a pie taller than the baker.

"She'll never get that to stand," said Mortimer, before he could stop himself.

Agnes looked at him sideways.

"She will if she chills the filling," she said.

"There isn't time to chill the filling."

"There's always time to chill the filling."

Griselda did not chill the filling. The pie went over sideways, slowly, like a tree in no particular hurry, and landed on the scarecrow.

Mortimer made a small noise inside the mask.

"Told you," he said.

"Oh, shush."

At the next commercial, he remembered who he was.

He stood up. He raised the shears. The lights flickered. Thunder cracked directly overhead, and with a fizz and a pop the whole house went dark.

The television died.

In the blackness, the Night Gardener's mask gleamed white in a flash of lightning, dimples and all.

"Now," he whispered. "Now, Agnes Pettibone, your time has come."

"My SHOW," said Agnes.

She surged out of the recliner with a fury he hadn't thought she had in her. She made it nearly two whole steps.

Then down she went, and her hand landed squarely on the big white button around her neck.

*BEEP.*

A calm voice came out of the button.

"Medical alert. Mrs. Pettibone, have you fallen?"

"Of course I've fallen," said Agnes, from the rug. "That's what the button's for."

"Are you hurt?"

"There's a man in my house with hedge shears and a doll's face, and the power's out in the middle of the showstopper."

There was a short pause on the line.

"Is he hurting you, Mrs. Pettibone?"

"He's hurting my evening."

"Help is on the way," said the voice.

Mortimer stood over her in the dark, uncertain. He had a strong feeling that the night was no longer going the way the night was supposed to go.

"Who's coming?" he said.

"Depends who's closest," said Agnes. "Pick me up, would you?"

He picked her up.

Out on the lane, something was coming down the road in the rain.

Slap. Slap. Slap.

Wet feet on wet asphalt.

"WHO," said the dark.

Mortimer turned toward the window.

"WOKE."

The porch light swung on its chain.

"ME."

On the church roof, Old Spout shook his head.

"Said it was the wrong house," he remarked to the weathervane. "Nobody listens."

The door, which was not locked, banged open.

The goose of Miller Creek stood on the welcome mat, dripping, with its neck low and its wings half open. Behind it, at a respectful distance, stood seven fishermen in rain slickers holding flashlights. They were on the neighborhood watch rota. They had been hoping for something like this for years.

The goose looked at the Night Gardener.

The Night Gardener looked at the goose.

"FEET," said the goose.

Mortimer looked down. He had not wiped them.

The goose came at him like a sack of wet laundry thrown from a great height.

He dropped the shears. He went over the coffee table, through the kitchen, out the back door, across the yard and straight into the hedge, which, for the first time in his career, he was actually close enough to garden.

"That's one for the scorebook," said the fisherman who kept score, writing it down.

The power came back on with a hum.

The television flickered to life, mid-sentence, on the final judging.

"Oh, thank goodness," said Agnes. "Turn it up."

Somebody did. It was Mortimer. He had come back in, wet, scratched and missing one sleeve, with the goose holding the other one. He'd just wanted to know who won.

On the screen, the small pumpkin with the crown rolled to the center of the table.

"Pie number twelve," he declared, "is a triumph."

Griselda burst into tears. The scarecrow, still covered in pie eleven, applauded.

Agnes clapped too. Then she looked up at the very damp serial killer standing on her rug.

"Well," she said. "You may as well sit down. It's still raining."

He sat.

The goose sat on his feet, so he wouldn't go anywhere. The fishermen let themselves out.

During the end credits, Agnes turned to him one more time.

"So why my house?"

Mortimer took a breath. For the first time, he was going to tell somebody.

"Next week's episode," said the announcer, "the gingerbread round!"

"Shh," said Agnes, and he never did get to say.

The Night Gardener came back the following Tuesday.

He knocked this time. He wiped his feet. He brought a pie. He'd chilled the filling.

Agnes fell down twice before the opening credits. He picked her up both times without being asked, and made the tea, and found the good cups behind the cereal.

The goose came too. It sat on his feet.

The papers stopped writing about the Night Gardener after that. The police took the red string off the corkboard. Nobody ever found out how he picked the houses.

The gargoyles knew, of course.

Nobody asked them.

After all, not every serial killer gets talked out of the business by a lady who wouldn't miss the semifinal.

But then again—

not every little old lady is Agnes Pettibone.

And not every little pumpkin judges pie on live television. 🎃🥧📺
