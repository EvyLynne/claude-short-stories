# Fred and the Last Slice

It was Halloween Town and the time of year when the fog smelled like butter. It became more pervasive at dusk as if the oncoming darkness fed the beast. It carried the scent of butter and sugar from one end of town and cloves from the other. By moonrise you could taste the air.

It was the exciting time of the festival.

Tables ran the length of the square. Ghosts fussed over meringues. Skeletons guarded their shortbread with their lives, such as they were. The mummy had brought a strudel and would not say what it was wrapped in.

What everyone agreed on was this: for one night, no one haunted anyone. The ghosts put down their chains. The witches put away their grudges.

Most of the witches.

Every oven in town had been lit since morning, and every chimney told on its owner. You could walk the length of Main Street with your eyes closed and know exactly who was making what, and who was going to lose.

At the far end, where the lantern light gave out, stood a thirteenth table that nobody remembered setting up. The gargoyles claimed to remember, but gargoyles' memories are notorious for being unreliable.

On it sat one pie.

It was dark, and glossy, and it steamed in a way that seemed to be looking around.

Behind it stood Griselda Wartwhistle in a false mustache.

"Free samples," she said. "From a stranger. Who is not banned."

Nobody in Halloween Town had ever refused a free sample.

Nobody knew who had held the first one but the slices went fast.

The zombies had two each. The Mayor had one, folded in half to fit. Farmer Grady, visiting for the parade, had a slice and said it tasted like being right about something.

Pip and Squeak arrived last, as the pan was nearly bare.

There was one slice left.

Pip took it. He always went first.

"Delicious," said Pip.

He waited.

Squeak pouted.

Fred did not eat any. Neither did Fluffy, nor Big Bertha, who was parked by the fountain in her parade ribbons.

Pumpkins do not eat pie. It is a matter of principle.

So the three of them were the first to notice when the square went sour.

It began small.

A ghost remarked that the skeletons' shortbread was dry. A skeleton replied that at least it was *there*, unlike some people.

The vampire recalled that the werewolf had borrowed a ladder in 1962.

Harold told Grady that an apology was nice but it didn't fix a hole in a shirt. Grady told Harold that twenty years of free hats ought to count for something.

"Stop copying me," Pip snapped at Squeak, who hadn't made a sound.

Sam bit the Mayor's ankle on general grounds.

Under the thirteenth table, a mug cracked down the middle.

Something climbed out of it, stretching.

Grudge was the size of a cat. Then a goat. He breathed in, and the whole square's bickering went up his nose like steam.

"Oh, that's lovely," he sighed. "Forty years of ladder. You can taste the oak."

Griselda peeled off her mustache, delighted.

"Baked right in," she told him proudly. "Every crumb. My best work."

"It was," Grudge agreed, now the size of the bakery. "I won't be needing you any more."

He picked her up by the hat and dropped her into her own empty pie pan, which fit her better than either of them expected.

"I'm BANNED from this," she said faintly. "I'm not even supposed to be in the pan."

Fred rolled into the middle of the square.

He had ended a curse once by forgiving a farmer. He knew how this worked.

"Everyone, listen," he called. "Whatever you're angry about, say sorry, and it stops."

Two zombies shook hands.

Three new arguments broke out over who had shaken first.

Grudge laughed until the lanterns swung.

"Go on, little princess. Mend them. There are four hundred of them and they've known each other for centuries. I'll wait." He patted his stomach. "I'm bigger than your tower. I'm bigger than your PUMPKIN."

"Rude," said Bertha.

"The more you fix, the more they remember. I can't be starved. There's always more."

Fred's painted smile had never felt so much like paint.

"He's right," Fluffy muttered. "You can't empty a town."

A small voice spoke from the rim of the fountain.

"Then don't."

Everyone turned.

It was Squeak. She was not repeating anybody. Nobody had said it first.

"He eats anger," she said. "So he's never once been full. What happens if he gets more than he can hold?"

"More than he can…?" said Pip, before he could stop himself.

It was the first time in his life he had been the echo.

"We'd need somebody angrier than a whole town," said Fluffy.

There was a pause while four hundred citizens and one farmer thought of the same individual.

Grady went pale. "No."

Fred looked up into the dark. "Holy Crow!"

It was not an exclamation.

The crow dropped onto Harold's hat and listened to the plan with one eye, then the other.

"Goose? No. No! NO."

He looked at the demon blotting out the moon.

"…Fine."

He was gone a long time.

When the candy corn gate finally creaked, the square fell silent. Even the arguing stopped, out of something like respect.

The goose walked in.

It did not hurry. It had never hurried for anyone. It came down the middle of the street with its neck low and its feet slapping the cobbles, and the fog got out of its way.

Grudge sniffed the air and his red eyes went wide.

"Oh," he breathed. "Oh, what IS that?"

"Don't," said Griselda from the pan. "I know that goose."

Grudge inhaled.

The goose was angry at the farmer who had landed in its creek. It was angry at the twelve decoys, each individually. It was angry at bridges, at Tuesdays, at bread that was thrown too far and bread that was not thrown far enough. It had been angry since the egg, and it had never forgiven the egg.

Grudge swelled past the rooftops.

"It doesn't END," he gasped. "Why doesn't it end?"

The goose hissed.

Grudge went taut as a drum, turned the color of a plum, and said, in a very small voice, "Too rich."

Then he popped.

It rained cinnamon for a full minute.

When it cleared, the square was full of people looking at their shoes. The werewolf went quietly to fetch a ladder.

On the cobblestones lay one burnt crumb with two tiny, furious eyes.

The goose ate it.

Nobody felt this was a solution, exactly. But the goose looked the same as before, which was to say furious, so it was impossible to tell.

It turned, found Farmer Grady in the crowd, and held his gaze for a long moment.

Then it walked home.

Fred rolled over to the pie pan.

"Griselda Wartwhistle. Why were you banned in the first place?"

She folded her arms. "Forty years ago I entered a pie. It bit a judge."

"That's all?"

"It was a small bite."

Fred considered.

"The ban is lifted," he said. "Next year you enter properly. One pie. Nothing in it but pie."

Griselda stared at him in horror.

"That's worse," she said. "Now I'll have to be *good* at it."

She climbed out of the pan, took out her chart, and tore it in half.

Then she put both halves in her pocket, in case.

Up on the fountain, Pip shuffled sideways along the rim.

"You can go first sometimes," he said.

Squeak thought about it.

"Sometimes," she agreed.

"Sometimes," said Pip, and found he didn't mind the sound of it.

That night, Fred sat atop the Grand Pumpkin Tower with cinnamon still drifting down like snow.

After all, not every little pumpkin can save his town by inviting the angriest creature alive to dinner.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And not every echo waits to be spoken to. 🎃🥧
