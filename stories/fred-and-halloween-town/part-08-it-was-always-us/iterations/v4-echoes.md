# It Was Always Us

Fred noticed it at breakfast.

He was sitting on top of the Grand Pumpkin Tower, watching the sun come up through the fog, when he realized he could also see it coming up through himself.

He looked down. He could see the stone beneath him. Not around him. Through him.

"Sam," he said carefully. "Do I look orange to you?"

Sam looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out one paw and pushed it straight through his middle.

Neither of them enjoyed that.

By lunchtime it was all over town. Princess Fred's crown had gone pale as a soap bubble. The candy corn gate had lost its yellow stripe. Every portrait of the coronation showed a crowd cheering at an empty cushion.

"It's a curse," said Harold, notebook out.

"It's a disease," said Fluffy.

"It's Grudge," said the bell tower. "He's got into the Grandfather."

Nobody listened. It was the gargoyles.

There were three of them, carved into the corners of the Halloween Town bell tower so long ago that nobody remembered who had put them there.

Grimsby was the eldest, with a face like a disappointed walnut. Lintel was the tall, thin one who thought he was funny. Doug was the small round one at the end, who waved at everything that went past, including weather.

They had watched over the town for three hundred years. In all that time they had been right about nearly everything, and nobody had listened to them once.

The thing they were talking about was worse.

Grudge was a demon. A small one, most days. He lived on resentment, and he grew fat on it, and every time Halloween Town had fallen to pieces in the past year, he had been somewhere close by, eating.

After the trouble with the goose, the town had finally caught him. He'd been pulled out, sealed in a glass jar and set on the highest ledge of the bell tower, where the gargoyles could keep an eye on him and tell him stories he couldn't walk away from.

"Is anyone else," said the Mayor, "worried that I can see the fountain through the princess?"

"He escaped the jar last night," Grimsby went on. "Crawled into the clock. Went back a year. Doug saw him go."

"I waved," said Doug.

"Last week," the Mayor told the crowd, "they said their cousin invented the moon."

Fred's leaf drifted off the top of his head, fell straight through him, and landed on the cobblestones.

There was a short silence.

"What's the Grandfather?" said Fred.

It was behind the bell. Of course it was.

It stood next to the empty spot where the demon puller used to be: a tall, dark grandfather clock with a pendulum like a sleepy eye and a face that read thirteen o'clock.

On the shelf beside it sat Grudge's jar, lid off, with a note inside in tiny, smug handwriting:

*Gone to make a pie. —G*

"A pie?" said Fluffy. "Out of what?"

Everyone looked at Fred. Fred looked at the note.

"Oh," said Fred.

Doug opened the clock door.

The inside seemed much bigger than the outside. Much bigger. It was a vast round room full of ticking, with brass levers, a dining table, a chandelier made of pocket watches, and several hundred cuckoos asleep in little doors around the walls.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then everybody moved at once.

Fluffy rolled a full lap of the room just to see if he could, and had to stop halfway for a rest. Pip and Squeak shot straight up to the chandelier and swung from it.

"Forever!" squeaked Squeak.

Pip blinked. He hadn't said anything yet.

"…It goes on forever," said Pip, a full second behind.

"Stop doing that."

"I can't help it," said Squeak. "It's the clock. Everything in here comes back early."

Somewhere above them, a cuckoo popped out of its door, said "Early!" and went back in.

Sam found a velvet armchair that had obviously been waiting for her for three hundred years and claimed it without discussion.

Harold stood in the middle of it all with his mouth open, turning slowly in a circle.

"There's a staircase," he said. "There's a staircase *in a clock*. There's a hat rack. There's a second hat rack, for spare hats. From the outside you'd think there was barely room for the pendulum."

"Most people never look inside," said Grimsby.

"I could live here," said Harold. "I could hold *meetings* here."

"Get in," said Doug. "We're late. Or early. One of those." He frowned at a dial. "Possibly both. I never can keep it straight. Don't think about it too hard."

Grimsby explained on the way, while Doug pulled levers and every cuckoo in the room woke up at once and began to argue.

The Grandfather had belonged to the gargoyles for three hundred years. On slow nights they used it to pop forward and see how things turned out.

"Which is why," said Grimsby, "we are always right."

"You read ahead," said Fluffy.

"Somebody has to. And every time we come back and tell you, nobody listens."

"Why do you think the thirteenth table moves?" said Lintel. "It's ours. It's a time table."

Nobody laughed. Lintel looked hurt.

"Where did Grudge go?" said Fred.

Doug tapped a dial. The hands spun backward, and outside the little round window, whole seasons went by in reverse: snow un-falling, leaves leaping back up onto branches, a pie un-baking itself in somebody's window.

"Exactly one year ago," said Doug. "The night a little pumpkin rolled off a farm."

"He wants Farmer Grady to catch me," said Fred.

"Precisely. Grady catches you. You become pie. Grady stays bitter for the rest of his life, and that's a feast for Grudge. No Princess Fred. No forgiveness. No jar."

"And me?" said Fred.

Doug looked at the faint orange outline sitting in the passenger seat.

"You're what we call 'being un-happened,'" he said gently. "It's quite fashionable."

The clock landed with a bong that rattled every tooth in the room.

They stepped out into a moonlit pumpkin patch.

It was the farm. It was the night. There was the barn, and the scarecrow on his pole, and the farmhouse with one light on upstairs.

And over in the patch, rocking very slowly back and forth, was a little orange pumpkin with a cheerful painted smile.

Fred stared at himself. He was solid, and bright, and so very young.

"Don't," said Grimsby, catching him by the stem. "You must never meet yourself. Terrible things happen."

"Like what?"

"Nobody knows. Nobody listens long enough for us to finish."

Harold cleared his throat and held up his notebook.

"Before we begin," he announced, "I am establishing rules. Rule one, the Hands-Off-History Rule. We touch nothing. We change nothing. We observe history and we let it—"

He stepped backward and knocked over a wheelbarrow, which rolled down the hill, through a fence and into the pond.

"—happen," Harold finished.

"Did that wheelbarrow always end up in the pond?" asked Fluffy.

"It did," said Doug, checking a pocket watch. "You're fine. Carry on."

The first problem was the chickens.

In the original story, Fred had rolled past the chicken coop and one chicken had squawked, "Good luck, Fred!" It had been the first kind thing anyone ever said to him.

Tonight the coop was padlocked shut. A tiny note hung from the latch: *Ha. —G*

Down the slope, young Fred had stopped rocking. He was looking at the dark field ahead, and he looked uncertain.

"He's going to give up," whispered Fred. "I remember how scared I was. That chicken was the only reason I kept going."

They all turned to Squeak.

"Absolutely not," said Squeak.

"Absolutely. She'll do it," said Pip.

It was the first time in his life he had echoed anybody, and he got it completely wrong on purpose.

Five minutes later, a very small chicken with a painted cardboard beak, three borrowed feathers and wings that were plainly bat wings flapped onto the fencepost.

"GOOD LUCK, FRED!" she squawked.

"Bawk," Pip added from behind the fence, for authenticity.

Young Fred brightened at once and rolled on.

Squeak came back and pulled off the beak.

"I always wondered why that chicken had such a high voice," said Fred.

"And why it flew off sideways," said Pip.

Squeak glared at him. "And why it had a backup singer."

The second problem was the sign.

Doug consulted his watch. "By sunrise, he'll roll through the woods and find a crooked sign. Halloween Town, This Way."

They ran ahead into the woods.

The dead tree was there. The sign was not.

There was nothing but a nail and another tiny note: *Oops. —G*

"Oh no," said Fluffy. "Who even put that sign there in the first place?"

The gargoyles all looked at him.

"Me?" said Fluffy.

"You," said Lintel. "Always have. Well, you will have. Well, you are about to have." He scratched his head with a claw. "I had that sentence and then I lost it."

So Fluffy painted a sign on a plank from the clock's spare parts cupboard.

He wrote HALLOWEEN TOWN in his best letters. He added This Way, and an arrow, and a tiny pumpkin for decoration.

Then he tried to hang it on the nail. Fluffy is a pumpkin. He has no arms and the nail was very high. After eleven attempts, the sign went up hanging from one corner at a terrible angle.

He stepped back and looked at it.

"Oh," said Fluffy quietly. "That's why it's crooked."

The third problem was Harold.

Dawn came up pink. Somewhere behind the farmhouse, a window flew open.

"FRED!"

Young Fred rolled for the cornfield. Farmer Grady came charging over the hill with his gleaming county-fair axe.

Present Fred watched from behind a hay bale, mouthing along to every word.

"In a minute he swings at me," Fred whispered. "He misses, and the axe hits Harold."

They all looked over at the scarecrow's spot in the field.

The pole was empty.

Young Harold was lying behind a fence, pulled out of the ground and tied up with twine. Another note was pinned to his hat: *Aim better. —G*

"With Harold gone," said Doug, "the axe hits something else."

Young Fred was zigzagging straight toward the empty spot.

Harold, present Harold, the one in the tuxedo, stood up.

"No," said Fluffy. "Harold, no."

"Twenty years I stood in that field," said Harold, straightening his tie. "I know my mark."

He sprinted, as well as a scarecrow can sprint, which is mostly falling forward on purpose, and planted himself on his old spot with his arms out, just as Farmer Grady swung.

THWACK!

The axe buried itself in Harold's chest.

"Sorry, Harold!" shouted Grady, yanking it free, and ran on after young Fred.

Harold staggered back to the hay bale, holding his straw together.

"For the record," he said faintly, "I've been telling him for a year it was in my chest. He kept saying it was an accident."

"It was," said Fred.

"It was ME," said Harold. "I volunteered. That's worse. That's so much worse."

The fourth problem was the bridge.

They raced ahead to Miller Creek, which was harder than it sounds when one of you is see-through and another is leaking hay.

Somebody had propped up the old rotten bridge with a brand-new beam. It was solid as a church. It would hold a farmer easily, and young Fred would be caught on the far side.

"Pip. Squeak," said Fred. "Teeth."

The bats went at the beam like two tiny, furious saws.

"Mmph mm mmph," said Pip, through a mouthful of wood.

"Mmph mm mmph!" squeaked Squeak.

"He says the beam tastes like a Tuesday," said Doug. "She says it tastes like a Tuesday somebody left out in the rain."

Everyone stared at him.

"Mmph mmph mm mmmph," said Pip.

"He says it's oak, and he'll be finished in a minute."

"Mmph mmph mm mmmph MMPH!"

"She says she'll be finished in *half* a minute, and he chews like a duck."

"How can you possibly understand that?" said Harold.

Doug shrugged. "I've stood on this bank and listened to them do it four hundred times. You pick it up."

"Mmph," said Squeak, with feeling.

"That one's rude," said Doug. "I won't translate it."

"I've always wondered," said Fluffy, "why this bridge waited a hundred years to fall down on exactly the right morning."

Pip spat out a splinter. "Now you know."

Squeak spat out a bigger one. "Now you *both* know. I chewed more."

They finished just as young Fred rolled across. The farmer thundered onto the planks behind him.

Crack. Crunch.

"Here it goes," said Fred, smiling his painted smile.

Nothing happened.

The bridge held. It creaked. It wobbled. It seemed to be thinking it over.

Farmer Grady was nearly across.

Then a voice spoke from under the boards: thin, oily, and very pleased.

"Hold steady, bridge. Hold steady. Hold a GRUDGE."

Grudge was underneath, clinging to the struts with both arms, holding the whole thing together by sheer bad attitude.

"You!" said Fred.

Grudge looked up and grinned with far too many teeth.

"You're fading, little princess. One more minute, he's across, and you're a filling."

Sam hissed and crouched to spring, but the gap was too wide.

Then something came up the creek bank.

Slap. Slap. Slap.

It was the goose.

It was not yet the goose they knew. It was a year younger. But it was already, unmistakably, the goose, and it was not having a good morning.

Grudge had been very busy that night. To clear the creek of witnesses, he had thrown the goose's breakfast bread into the next county.

It had been thrown far too far.

The goose stopped. It looked at the bridge. It looked under the bridge. It saw a small rotten thing clinging there, smelling of stale crumbs.

"YOU," said the goose. "BREAD."

"Now, wait," said Grudge.

The goose bit him.

Grudge let go of the bridge with both arms.

CRAAAAACK!

The bridge, which had been waiting a hundred years to do this, gave way all at once.

"Timber," said Pip.

"Timber!" squeaked Squeak.

The echo bounced off the water, ran down the creek, and came back from seven fishermen at once: "TIMBER!"

Then from the goose: "TIM. BER."

Then from Farmer Grady, in midair: "*Timberrrr—*"

SPLASH.

Farmer Grady went into the creek in the middle of the fishing tournament, and so did Grudge, and so did the goose, which landed on Grudge.

"THE GOOSE WAS ESPECIALLY UPSET," said the fisherman who kept score, writing it down.

Seven fishermen and twelve duck decoys surrounded the farmer. The goose surrounded Grudge.

Up on the bank, Fred watched a tiny orange pumpkin roll safely away into the woods, toward a crooked sign.

He looked down at himself.

His crown was silver again. His leaf was green. He was orange, solid, and very slightly damp.

"I'm back," said Fred.

"You were always back," said Doug. "Or you always were going to be. Or you are now, which counts." He paused. "I've confused myself. Let's go home."

Grudge crawled out of the creek, dripping, missing a good deal of himself, and found Lintel standing over him holding a glass jar.

It was labeled. The label was very old and very faded. It said *GRUDGE. RETURN TO BELL TOWER.*

"How long have you had that?" spluttered Grudge.

"Three hundred years," said Lintel. "We told you this would happen."

"When?"

"Tuesday. And before that, every Tuesday since the Renaissance. You never listen."

The lid went on with a click.

Before they left, Fred stood at the edge of the woods for one last look.

Young Fred had stopped beneath the crooked sign. He was reading it slowly, with his whole face, the way you read something that might change your life.

Present Fred wanted very badly to roll over and tell him everything.

That he'd be crowned. That he'd have a cat with opinions, two bats who couldn't stop finishing each other's sentences, and a best friend full of dandelion fluff. That the farmer with the axe would one day wheel a goose to his door and ask for help.

"You can't meet yourself," Grimsby reminded him gently.

"I know," said Fred.

Then young Fred turned his head, just for a second, as if somebody had said his name.

Present Fred didn't say a word. He just smiled.

Young Fred smiled back, at nobody he could see, and rolled off toward Halloween Town.

"Did I always do that?" Fred asked.

"Every time," said Doug.

The clock brought them home at thirteen o'clock, on the dot.

The candy corn gate had its stripes back. The coronation portraits had a pumpkin in them again, looking slightly embarrassed. The jar went back on its ledge, and the gargoyles settled down beside it.

"Right," said Lintel to Grudge. "Where were we? Ah. My cousin and the moon."

That night, Fred sat atop the Grand Pumpkin Tower with Sam on one side, Fluffy on the other, and two bats circling overhead.

"All clear," said Pip.

"All clear!" squeaked Squeak.

They flew on. Then, from somewhere very far away and very long ago, a third voice drifted over the rooftops, small and high and a little out of breath:

"…all clear!"

Pip and Squeak stopped in midair and looked at each other.

"Was that—"

"Me," said Squeak. "From last year. I always wondered where that one went."

"Doug," Fred called up to the bell tower. "If you can see the future, what happens next?"

The gargoyle leaned over the edge.

"Big Bertha sneezes Thursday," he said. "Wear a hat."

Nobody listened.

On Thursday, everybody needed a hat.

After all, not every little pumpkin gets to go back and make sure his own story happens.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And it turns out it was always us. 🎃🕰️
