# Fred and the Scarecrow Curse

*Written by Evelyn, with assistance from Claude (AI)*

*Fred and Halloween Town, Part 3. Version 4 of 4: see the [prompt log](prompt-log.md) for how it was made.*

Farmer Grady had not slept well in a year.

Every night he dreamed of one small, smiling pumpkin rolling away.

So one foggy October evening, he marched into the woods to find him.

He carried Harold, his scarecrow, over one shoulder. Grady told himself it was for company.

He followed a crooked sign.

He passed through a gate made of candy corn.

And on the other side, Harold got heavier.

Then Harold wriggled.

Then Harold said, “You can put me down now.”

Grady dropped him.

Harold landed on his two wooden posts, wobbled, and stood up straight for the first time in his life.

“Twenty years on a pole,” said Harold, stretching his straw arms. “You have no idea how much my back hurts.”

“You can talk,” said Grady.

“Everyone can talk here. Try to keep up.”

So they walked into Halloween Town side by side, the farmer stomping and the scarecrow taking careful, creaky steps like a newborn deer.

“For the record,” said Harold, “I remember the axe.”

“That was an accident.”

“It was in my chest, Grady.”

They were still arguing when something in the shadows opened one red eye.

It was a demon. A small one. Its name was Grudge.

Grudge lived wherever nobody looked: under the bakery stairs, behind the post office, inside the mayor’s spare top hat.

Grudge is frequently found to be that intangible darkness that takes over when anger evaporates.

Here at Halloween Town Grudge had a more tangible existence.

Grudge ate resentment.

And here came two helpings, bickering down the street.

“A grumpy farmer and a scarecrow with a grievance,” the demon whispered. “Delicious.”

It snapped its fingers.

Grady’s left thumb turned to straw.

Harold’s right hand turned pink and grew fingernails.

They both stared.

“Give that back,” said Harold.

“I didn’t take it!”

Then Grady’s elbow went stiff, and Harold’s sleeve wrapped around it, and neither one could pull away.

They were sticking together.

“No,” said Grady. “No, no, no.”

“I just got legs,” said Harold. “I am NOT sharing them.”

Grady pulled left. Harold pulled right.

Grady’s boots filled with hay. Harold’s burlap cheek sprouted a gray whisker.

They ran for the gate, three legs between them and no agreement on which one went first.

They fell over a pumpkin cart.

“Your knee is in my stuffing!” shouted Harold.

“Your stuffing is in my ear!” shouted Grady.

Harold grabbed a lamppost with his straw hand. Grady grabbed it with his real one. They held on while the curse pulled them closer, stitch by stitch.

“HELP!” they shouted, at exactly the same time, in nearly the same voice.

High above, two bats spotted them.

“Human in distress, town square,” reported Pip.

“Town square!” squeaked Squeak.

“Also a scarecrow. Possibly the same person.”

“Same person!” squeaked Squeak.

They flew straight to the Grand Pumpkin Tower.

Princess Fred was back from vacation, and he rolled down at once, with Sam padding beside him and Fluffy grumbling behind.

Fred stopped.

He knew that hat. He knew that beard. He knew that plaid shirt with the patch on the front.

“Farmer Grady? Harold?”

Two faces looked down, already sharing one nose.

“You,” croaked Grady.

The nose turned into a carrot stub.

“Stop being angry!” said Harold. “Every time you scowl, I get a mustache!”

“Every time YOU sulk, I get straw in my socks!”

“He’s right, you know,” said Fluffy. “Both of them. I’m not a doctor, but that seems important.”

The Mayor arrived, all twelve feet of him, and studied the tangle closely.

“This is not how scarecrows are made,” he said. “We are stuffed with care. These two are being stitched with spite.”

Sam’s fur stood on end.

Cats can see things others can’t.

She stared at the shadow under the lamppost and growled.

“Demon,” said Fred. “Pip, Squeak, find it!”

The bats swept the square, squeaking into every dark corner.

“Echo off something small and rotten, behind the fountain,” said Pip.

“Rotten!” squeaked Squeak.

Sam pounced.

Grudge shot out of the shadows, hissing, twice the size it had been an hour ago.

“You can’t stop it,” the demon sneered. “One hates the pumpkin. One hates the farmer. By midnight they’ll be a single scarecrow, and it will be mine.”

Grady fought.

He clenched the one fist he had left and dug his real heel into the cobblestones.

Harold fought too.

“I waited twenty years to walk,” he said through gritted stitches. “I will not spend the next twenty as someone’s left side.”

He braced his post against the fountain and heaved.

They strained in opposite directions until the seams between them creaked.

It wasn’t enough.

Fred rolled right up to their boots. All three of them.

“Farmer Grady,” he said, “I’m not angry at you.”

The farmer blinked.

“You wanted to make me into pie.”

“I did,” Grady whispered.

“And I got a kingdom out of it. So thank you, in a way.”

Grady’s half-burlap chin trembled.

“I measured you every morning,” he said. “I never once looked at you.”

He turned his stiff neck toward the face beside his own.

“And Harold. Twenty years you stood in my field in the rain, and I never said thank you. Then I hit you with an axe. I’m sorry.”

Harold was quiet for a moment.

“I kept the crows off your corn every single day,” he said.

“I know.”

“Even the big one. Gerald.”

“I know. You were the best scarecrow in the county.”

Harold sniffed, which was new for him.

“Apology accepted,” he said. “But I’m keeping the hat.”

Grudge shrieked.

It shrank to the size of a cat. Then a chicken. Then a candy corn.

The seams popped open. Straw flew out of Grady’s sleeves. His thumb turned pink. His knees remembered which way to bend.

Harold tumbled free, checked that he had two arms and no whiskers, and stood up on his own two posts.

Sam swatted the tiny demon across the square.

It landed with a plop in a steaming mug held by Griselda Wartwhistle, who was working the free pumpkin spice stand.

She put a lid on it.

The mug rattled. Something inside whispered her name.

Griselda looked left. She looked right. Nobody was watching the witch at the drink stand. Nobody ever did.

She lifted the lid, just a crack, and breathed in.

It smelled like every bake sale she’d been banned from.

A slow, crooked smile spread across her face, the first real one in months.

“Free. Forever,” she muttered. “We’ll see about that.”

She tucked the mug deep inside her cloak and went back to pouring, humming a tune that made the nearest lantern flicker.

Farmer Grady sat on the edge of the fountain, entirely human and entirely tired.

The Mayor offered Harold a job, and a tuxedo.

“Does it come with a pole?” asked Harold.

“Certainly not.”

“Then I accept.”

Fred and Harold walked the farmer to the candy corn gate together.

“Come back for the Moonlight Parade,” said Fred. “You can judge the pumpkins.”

“By size?” asked Grady.

Fred smiled his familiar smile.

“By heart.”

Grady shook Harold’s straw hand, properly this time, and walked home lighter than he’d come.

One piece of straw was still stuck in his beard.

He left it there.

After all, not every little pumpkin can forgive the farmer who chased him with an axe.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred. 🎃🌾
