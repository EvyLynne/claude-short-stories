# The Quiet on Miller Creek

The seven fishermen of Miller Creek had the best morning of their lives, and it frightened them badly.

Nobody's line was bitten through. Nobody's hat was taken. Nobody was chased up the bank, across the road and into a hedge.

They caught eleven trout between them, in perfect peace, and went home early with the doors locked.

The frogs had not sung for a week. The reeds stood very straight. Even the water seemed to be minding its manners.

Up at the farm, Farmer Grady stood at his fence looking at three days of bread.

He threw it every morning. He always threw it badly, a little too far or not quite far enough, and every morning it was gone by noon, along with a piece of his trouser leg.

Now it lay where it had landed, going green.

He walked down to the creek with his heart behaving oddly.

The goose was sitting on the bank with its feet tucked under, watching nothing.

It turned its head. Its eyes had a dull red glow, like coals under ash.

"GOOD," said the goose. "MORNING."

Grady dropped his coffee.

There was only one way to send word to the other side, and he hated it.

He walked out to the middle of his cornfield, stuck out both arms, and stood there like a scarecrow.

It took eleven minutes.

"Farmer!" cawed Holy Crow, landing on his hat with enormous satisfaction. "On pole! HA!"

"It's the goose," said Grady.

"Goose. What. Now."

"It said good morning."

The crow went still.

"It let the fishermen fish. I walked right past it. It said, 'Mind. The. Mud.'"

Holy Crow looked toward the creek, then at Grady, then at the creek again.

"Bad," he said, quite softly, for him. "Bring."

Grady fetched the wheelbarrow.

He lined it with a horse blanket, which he told himself was to protect the wheelbarrow. Then he lifted the goose in with both arms.

It did not bite him. It weighed less than its feathers ought to.

He pushed it up the farm road and into the woods at dusk, past the crooked sign, with the wheel squeaking and the goose riding in front like a small, pale passenger.

At the gate made of candy corn, it lifted its head.

"PRETTY," said the goose.

Grady walked faster.

Halloween Town came out to look, and wished it hadn't.

The zombies stood back. The ghosts dimmed. The gargoyles announced they had seen this coming, and nobody asked them.

Sam took one look into the wheelbarrow and every hair on her body stood up.

"It's him," said Fred. "Isn't it."

Griselda Wartwhistle came out of her kitchen with flour to the elbows and a rolling pin in her fist. She had been practicing. It was going poorly.

She bent over the goose and listened to its chest the way you'd listen at a door.

"He's not feasting on a town this time," she said. "He's got one goose, and he's eating it slow, from the middle out. Every grudge it ever held. When it's empty, he'll wear it."

"How long?" said Grady.

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Then we fill it back up," said a small voice.

It was Squeak. It was her turn to go first.

"Back up!" said Pip.

"It was the angriest thing alive. So make it angry again."

The town tried everything.

Harold threw a loaf of bread clear over the bakery.

"THANK. YOU," said the goose.

The Mayor declared that it was Tuesday. It was Friday.

"LOVELY."

They borrowed twelve duck decoys from the fishermen and floated them in the fountain.

The goose regarded them for some time.

"FRIENDS," it said.

A vampire fainted. Big Bertha rolled directly in front of it and blocked its entire view of everything.

"TAKE. YOUR. TIME."

"Stop," said Grady. "Stop it. All of you."

He said it so sharply that even Holy Crow shut his beak.

Fred rolled up beside the wheelbarrow.

"You can't make somebody angry on purpose," he said. "I don't think it works that way. I think you can only stay."

So Grady stayed.

The square emptied. The lanterns burned down. An old farmer sat on the edge of the fountain with a goose in a horse blanket, and for a long while neither of them said anything.

"I throw that bread wrong on purpose," Grady said at last.

The goose blinked.

"Have done for years. Figured you liked having something to holler about. Figured I did too."

He rubbed the back of his neck.

"My whole life, I measured things. Pumpkins. Corn. Other men's fields. You were the only creature in the county who never cared what size I was. You just came at me, honest, every single morning."

His voice had gone rough.

"I'd take the hedge again. I'd take it every day."

The goose laid its long neck across his knee.

"TIRED," it said.

Then its beak opened, wider than a beak should, and something poured out of it like smoke from a chimney.

Grudge rose over the fountain, fat and glossy and finished.

"Every last drop," he sighed. "The egg. The bridges. Tuesdays. I thought it would never end, and then one morning it did."

The goose lay still in the blanket.

The demon turned his red eyes on the farmer and licked his lips.

"And now you. I remember you. A whole year of grumbling over one little pumpkin. You were a banquet."

He breathed in.

He frowned, and breathed in again.

"Where is it?"

"Gave it up," said Grady. "Pumpkin's fault."

"Nobody gives it ALL up."

"Took some doing."

Grudge's face twisted. He was enormous, and he was hungry, and for the first time there was nothing on the table.

He caught Grady by the suspenders and lifted him off his feet.

"Then I'll squeeze some out of you."

In the wheelbarrow, one eye opened.

It was not red. It was small, and black, and very clear.

"MINE," said the goose.

It stood up.

"MY," it said, stepping onto the fountain's edge. "FARMER."

Grudge laughed and inhaled, ready for one more helping.

Nothing came.

"What is that?" he choked. "It isn't old. It isn't *held*. It's brand new, it's happening right now, I can't—"

The goose hit him like a sack of wet laundry thrown from a great height.

It bit him on the nose. It bit him on several things that might have been elbows. It drove a demon the size of a bakery three times around the square, hissing like a kettle, while shutters banged open and four hundred citizens leaned out to watch.

Grudge shrank as he ran. There was nothing to eat, and running is hungry work.

By the third lap he was the size of a plum. He threw himself at Griselda's feet.

"The mug!" he squealed. "Take me back! You and me, the bake sale, the chart, think of the CHART!"

Griselda looked down at him for a long time.

Her hand went to her apron pocket, where two halves of a torn piece of paper had been riding around for a week.

The square held its breath.

"Do you know what I've learned about grudges?" she said. "Forty years I carried mine. I thought it was carrying me."

She took her hand out of her pocket. It was empty. She held it open for him to see.

"You're only as big as whoever's holding you."

Grudge spun around.

The zombies were not holding anything. Neither were the ghosts, or the werewolf, who had returned the ladder. Harold had his hand on the Mayor's shoulder. Pip and Squeak sat wing to wing.

Grady stood by the fountain, straightening his suspenders.

Nobody in the square was holding him at all.

Grudge opened his mouth to say something clever.

He was the size of a candy corn. Then a crumb. Then a faint smell of burnt sugar.

Then the fog smelled only of butter.

Griselda went back inside. A moment later, two halves of a chart went into the oven, and something in there finally rose properly.

In the square, the goose turned on the crowd and hissed at every single one of them.

They cheered. It hissed louder. It was the best welcome Halloween Town had ever given anyone.

Fred saw them off at the gate at sunrise.

"You'll come back for the parade?" he asked.

"If I'm allowed out," said Grady, nodding at the wheelbarrow.

The goose bit him on the thumb.

He had never been so glad of anything in his life.

That evening the frogs of Miller Creek began to sing again, carefully, in case.

In the morning a piece of bread sailed over the fence and landed much too far away.

"LATER," the goose had told him once.

It turned out later was every day.

After all, not every little pumpkin would open his gates to a farmer who once chased him with an axe, and tell him the only cure was to stay.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And not every enemy is only an enemy. 🎃🪿
