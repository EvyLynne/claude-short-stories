# The Quiet on Miller Creek

Miller Creek had rules, and the goose made them.

Everybody who lived along the water knew it. The seven fishermen knew it best. They were the same seven who had been holding a tournament the day Farmer Grady fell through the bridge and landed in the middle of it, and they had fished there every Saturday since, under strict supervision.

The supervision bit through their lines. It took their hats. Most weeks it chased at least one of them up the bank, across the road and into a hedge.

They had come to think of it as part of the sport. One of them kept score.

So when a whole Saturday went by without so much as a hiss, the seven fishermen did not enjoy it.

They caught eleven trout between them. Nobody bothered them at all. It was so peaceful that by noon they couldn't stand it any longer, and they packed up and hurried home, looking over their shoulders the whole way.

The frogs had noticed too.

Every evening for as long as the creek could remember, the frogs had sung at dusk. It wasn't music. It was a warning: *goose on patrol, goose on patrol.*

For a week now there had been nothing to warn anybody about.

So the frogs sat in the reeds, silent, and did not trust it.

Up at the farm, Farmer Grady stood at his fence looking at three days of bread.

The bread was a toll.

Ever since the night in Halloween Town when the goose had looked him in the eye and said "LATER," it had turned up at his fence every morning at sunrise to collect.

Grady paid in bread. He always threw it badly, a little too far or not quite far enough, because the goose seemed to enjoy having something to complain about.

The goose would eat the bread. Then, for the matter of the creek, it would march over and tear a piece out of his trouser leg.

Grady owned eleven pairs of trousers. None of them had a complete left cuff.

Now three mornings' worth of bread lay in the grass, going green, and his trousers were in the best condition they'd been in for a year.

He walked down to the creek to see what was wrong.

The goose was standing on the bank. When it saw him, it stepped aside to let him pass.

"GOOD," said the goose. "MORNING."

Grady dropped his coffee.

"NICE," the goose added. "TROUSERS."

Then its stomach spoke.

"Is somebody there?" said a small, muffled, furious voice from somewhere under the feathers. "Hello? I'm STUCK."

There was only one way to send word to the other side, and Grady hated it.

He walked out to the middle of his cornfield, stuck out both arms, and stood there like a scarecrow.

It took eleven minutes.

"Farmer!" cawed Holy Crow, landing on his hat with enormous satisfaction. "On pole! HA!"

"It's the goose," said Grady.

"Goose. What. Now."

"It said good morning. It held the gate for me. It let a duckling go first."

The crow went still.

"Also its stomach is complaining. In sentences."

Holy Crow looked toward the creek, then at Grady, then at the creek again.

"Bad," he said. "Bring."

Grady fetched the wheelbarrow.

He pushed the goose up the farm road and into the woods at dusk, past the crooked sign, with the wheel squeaking and the goose riding in front, nodding pleasantly at the trees.

"This is undignified," said its stomach.

"You're in a goose," said Grady. "That ship has sailed."

At the gate made of candy corn, the goose lifted its head.

"PRETTY," it said.

Grady walked faster.

Halloween Town came out to look, and wished it hadn't.

The goose greeted the zombies. It complimented the Mayor's posture. It told the mummy that beige was a good color on him.

"I liked it better angry," whispered Fluffy. "I knew where I stood. Behind something."

Griselda Wartwhistle came out of her kitchen with flour to the elbows. She had been practicing honest pie for a week. So far she had produced nine, and four of them were in use around town as doorstops.

She put her ear to the goose.

"Wartwhistle?" said the stomach. "Is that you? Get me OUT of here."

"Grudge?"

"I ate all of it! Every grudge this bird ever had! The egg, the bridges, Tuesdays, I finished the lot, and now I'm too fat to fit back up the neck!"

"So the goose is polite because…"

"Because there's nothing left in it but manners! It's unbearable! It said 'pardon me' to a *rock*!"

Griselda straightened up.

"He's wedged," she told the square. "Somebody will have to pull him out."

"With what?" said Harold.

Nobody knew.

Griselda offered the goose a slice of pie number ten while they thought about it.

It was gray. It had sunk in the middle. A fork placed in it earlier had not been recovered.

The goose took one bite.

"DELICIOUS."

"It's worse than I feared," said Griselda.

"We have a demon puller," said a gravelly voice from the rooftops.

Everyone looked up.

The gargoyles looked back, pleased with themselves.

"Of course you do," sighed the Mayor. "Last week you had a cousin who invented the moon."

"We've had it three hundred years. It's in the bell tower. Behind the bell."

"You also claim to have seen the thirteenth table leave."

"It went north," said a gargoyle. "On its own legs."

The square groaned. Nobody had believed a gargoyle since before the candy corn gate was built.

"Has anyone ever checked?" said a small voice.

It was Squeak. It was her turn to go first.

"Ever checked?" said Pip.

Fred looked at the Mayor. The Mayor looked at Harold. Harold looked in his notebook, which was no help.

In three hundred years, nobody had once gone to look.

"Pip. Squeak," said Fred. "The bell tower."

They were back in under a minute, towing something between them on a rope.

It was real.

It was brass, and enormous, and looked like the offspring of a butter churn and a trombone. It had a crank on one side, a funnel on the other, and a rubber plunger on the end of a long chain. A small plaque read: PULLER, DEMON. FOR PERSONS, BEASTS, FOWL AND SUNDRY. POINT AWAY FROM FACE.

"Told you," said every gargoyle at once.

The instructions were engraved on the handle. There were three.

*One. Apply plunger to the afflicted. Mouth, beak, snout or muzzle. If none, improvise.*

"It's got a beak," said Harold, checking. "We're in luck."

The goose opened wide without being asked.

"THANK. YOU," it said, around the plunger.

*Two. Crank until the demon objects.*

Farmer Grady cranked. The machine wheezed. The chain went tight.

"Ow," said the stomach. "OW. That's my ear. I think that's my ear."

"It's objecting," said Fluffy.

*Three. Everybody pull.*

Grady took hold of the chain. Harold took hold of Grady. The Mayor took hold of Harold, and the zombies took hold of the Mayor, and the ghosts took hold of the zombies as well as ghosts can.

Big Bertha anchored the end of the line. Sam sat on top of Bertha and supervised.

Pip and Squeak each grabbed a link and flapped as hard as they could, which helped nobody and made them feel wonderful.

"On three," called Fred. "One. Two—"

"WAIT," said the stomach. "I've changed my mind, it's cozy, I'll stay, I'll pay RENT—"

"Three!"

They heaved.

The goose's neck stretched. The chain hummed like a fiddle string. Something inside went *thup, thup, thup*, like a boot coming out of mud.

POP.

Grudge came out of the beak like a cork out of a bottle.

He was as round as a dumpling. He sailed the length of the square, bounced off the bell, and dropped straight into the puller's funnel, which swallowed him with a satisfied *glunk*.

A glass jar slid out of the bottom, sealed, with a demon in it.

The whole tug-of-war fell over backward into a heap.

"Sorry," said a ghost, from somewhere inside a zombie.

In the wheelbarrow, the goose shut its beak.

It blinked.

It looked at Farmer Grady, lying on his back among the zombies with his trousers, for once, entirely intact.

It climbed down. It walked over. It selected the left cuff with great care, and tore off a piece.

"There you are!" cried Grady.

"BREAD," said the goose. "TOO. FAR."

"I know. I'll keep doing it."

"GOOD."

The square cheered. The goose hissed at them. They cheered louder.

That left the jar.

"Wartwhistle!" Grudge shouted through the glass. "The mug! You and me! Think of the chart!"

"Can't," said Griselda, sniffing the air. "Number eleven's rising. I think I've finally got a crust."

She went inside and shut the door.

"We'll take him," said the gargoyles.

They set the jar on the highest ledge of the bell tower, where they could keep an eye on it.

Then, having three hundred years of stories that nobody had ever stayed to hear, and a listener who could not leave, they began.

"It all started," said the first gargoyle, "with my cousin and the moon."

Grudge put his face against the glass.

He hated it.

Fred saw the farmer off at the gate at sunrise.

"You'll come back for the parade?"

"If I'm allowed out," said Grady, nodding at the wheelbarrow.

The goose hissed at Fred, at the gate, at the sunrise and at a passing leaf.

That evening the frogs of Miller Creek began to sing again, loud and clear: *goose on patrol, goose on patrol.*

The seven fishermen heard it from their porches and smiled. On Saturday, three of them went into the hedge. It was a wonderful day.

In the morning a piece of bread sailed over the fence and landed much too far away.

Grady was down to ten good pairs of trousers by the end of the week.

After all, not every little pumpkin would think to ask whether anyone had ever checked.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And every three hundred years or so, the gargoyles are right. 🎃🪿
