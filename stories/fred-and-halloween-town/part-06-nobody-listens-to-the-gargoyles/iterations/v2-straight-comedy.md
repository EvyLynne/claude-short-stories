# The Quiet on Miller Creek

The seven fishermen of Miller Creek had the best morning of their lives, and it frightened them badly.

Nobody's line was bitten through. Nobody's hat was taken. Nobody was chased up the bank, across the road and into a hedge.

They caught eleven trout between them, in perfect peace, and went home early with the doors locked.

The frogs had not sung for a week. They didn't trust it.

Up at the farm, Farmer Grady stood at his fence looking at three days of bread.

He threw it every morning. He always threw it badly, a little too far or not quite far enough, and every morning it was gone by noon, along with a piece of his trouser leg.

Now it lay where it had landed, going green.

He walked down to the creek to see what was wrong.

The goose was standing on the bank. When it saw him, it stepped aside to let him pass.

"GOOD," said the goose. "MORNING."

Grady dropped his coffee.

"NICE," the goose added. "HAT."

Then its stomach spoke.

"Is somebody there?" said a small, muffled, furious voice from somewhere under the feathers. "Hello? I'm STUCK."

There was only one way to send word to the other side, and Grady hated it.

He walked out to the middle of his cornfield, stuck out both arms, and stood there like a scarecrow.

It took eleven minutes.

"Farmer!" cawed Holy Crow, landing on his hat with enormous satisfaction. "On pole! HA!"

"It's the goose," said Grady.

"Goose. What. Now."

"It said good morning. It held the gate for me. It let a duckling go first."

The crow went still.

"Also its stomach is complaining. In sentences."

Holy Crow looked toward the creek, then at Grady, then at the creek again.

"Bad," he said. "Bring."

Grady fetched the wheelbarrow.

He pushed the goose up the farm road and into the woods at dusk, past the crooked sign, with the wheel squeaking and the goose riding in front, nodding pleasantly at the trees.

"This is undignified," said its stomach.

At the gate made of candy corn, the goose lifted its head.

"PRETTY," it said.

Grady walked faster.

Halloween Town came out to look, and wished it hadn't.

The goose greeted the zombies. It complimented the Mayor's posture. It told the mummy that beige was a good color on him.

"I liked it better angry," whispered Fluffy. "I knew where I stood. Behind something."

Griselda Wartwhistle came out of her kitchen with flour to the elbows. She had been practicing honest pie for a week. So far she had produced nine, and the gargoyles were using four of them as doorstops.

She put her ear to the goose.

"Wartwhistle?" said the stomach. "Is that you? Get me OUT of here."

"Grudge?"

"I ate all of it! Every grudge this bird ever had! The egg, the bridges, Tuesdays, I finished the lot, and now I'm too fat to fit back up the neck!"

"So the goose is polite because…"

"Because there's nothing left in it but manners! It's unbearable! It said 'pardon me' to a *rock*!"

Griselda straightened up.

"He's wedged," she told the square. "He'll only come out the way he went in. With a great deal of anger behind him."

"Then we make it angry again," said a small voice.

It was Squeak. It was her turn to go first.

"Angry again!" said Pip.

The town tried everything.

Harold threw a loaf of bread clear over the bakery.

"THANK. YOU," said the goose.

"Won't work," said the stomach. "I ate bread."

The Mayor declared that it was Tuesday. It was Friday.

"LOVELY."

"Ate Tuesdays. Keep up."

They borrowed twelve duck decoys from the fishermen and floated them in the fountain.

The goose regarded them for some time.

"FRIENDS," it said.

A vampire fainted.

Big Bertha rolled directly in front of the goose and blocked its entire view of everything.

"TAKE. YOUR. TIME."

Sam walked up and bit it on the tail. The goose apologized to her.

In desperation, Griselda brought out pie number ten.

It was gray. It had sunk in the middle. A fork placed in it earlier had not been recovered.

The goose took one bite.

"DELICIOUS."

"Now I'm worried," said Griselda.

"I throw the bread wrong on purpose!" Grady burst out.

Everyone looked at him.

"Every morning! Too far or too short! I figured it liked having something to holler about!" He turned to the goose. "There. Doesn't that make you mad?"

The goose considered him warmly.

"THOUGHTFUL," it said.

Grady sat down on the edge of the fountain and put his hat over his face.

It was at this moment that a man in waders wandered into the square.

He had a fishing rod over one shoulder and the look of someone who had been lost for a very long time.

Nobody had seen him since the tree sneezed him out. He had been asking for directions ever since and had not once been listened to.

"'Scuse me," he said. "I'm looking for a creek."

He noticed the wheelbarrow.

"Oh, hey," said the fisherman. "Whose duck is that?"

The square went silent.

The lanterns flickered.

Up on the rooftops, the gargoyles covered their eyes, and for once nobody doubted them.

The goose turned its head, very slowly, all the way around.

"WHAT."

"The duck," said the fisherman helpfully. "Big fella. Is he friendly?"

Something began to rumble deep inside the goose, like a kettle remembering what it was for.

"Oh no," said the stomach. "Oh no, that's a NEW one, I haven't eaten that one, there's no room, there's no—"

"DUCK?" said the goose.

"HONK."

Grudge came out of the beak like a cork out of a bottle.

He shot across the square, bounced off the bell, and landed in the fountain among the decoys, round as a dumpling and steaming faintly.

The goose did not even look at him.

It was looking at the fisherman.

"I. AM. A. GOOSE."

"Sure," said the fisherman. "Sorry. They look alike."

That was his second mistake.

The goose chased him twice around the square, through the bakery, over the thirteenth table, which had reappeared for the occasion, and out through the candy corn gate, which is how he finally found his way home.

Then it came back and bit Grady on the thumb.

"Ow!" said Grady, delighted. "There you are!"

"BREAD," said the goose. "TOO. FAR."

"I know. I'll keep doing it."

"GOOD."

In the fountain, Grudge was trying to climb out. He was too round to manage it.

"Wartwhistle! The mug! You and me! Think of the chart!"

"Can't," said Griselda, sniffing the air. "Number eleven's rising. I think I've finally got a crust."

She went inside and shut the door.

Grudge looked around for anywhere to hide.

He squeezed himself into the nearest duck decoy and pulled the tail shut behind him.

The next morning, the fishermen came to collect their property.

They took all twelve decoys home to Miller Creek, where the fishing was calm, the company was pleasant, and nobody resented anybody about anything.

It was the most peaceful place in the county.

He hated it.

Fred saw the farmer off at the gate.

"You'll come back for the parade?"

"If I'm allowed out," said Grady, nodding at the wheelbarrow.

The goose hissed at Fred, at the gate, at the sunrise and at a passing leaf.

All of Halloween Town came out to be hissed at. It was the warmest send-off anyone could remember.

That evening the frogs of Miller Creek began to sing again, carefully, in case.

In the morning a piece of bread sailed over the fence and landed much too far away.

After all, not every little pumpkin can end a crisis by letting a lost fisherman ask one question.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And not every duck is a duck. 🎃🪿
