# Nobody Listens to the Gargoyles

On the roof of the old stone church at Miller Creek sat a gargoyle called Old Spout.

He had been up there two hundred years, and he had been right about nearly all of them.

He had said the bridge was rotten. He said it for eleven years, right up until the morning a farmer went through it.

He had said the weathervane was loose, the bell rope was fraying, and the new minister would not last.

The people of Miller Creek thought he was the wind in the gutters.

“There’s something wrong with that goose,” said Old Spout, one Saturday in October.

Nobody listened.

Down on the water, the seven fishermen were having the best morning of their lives.

They were the same seven who had been holding a tournament the day Farmer Grady came through the bridge and landed in the middle of it. They had fished the creek every Saturday since, under the strict supervision of the goose.

The supervision bit through their lines. It took their hats. Most weeks it chased at least one of them up the bank, across the road and into a hedge.

They had come to think of it as part of the sport. One of them kept score.

Today there was not so much as a hiss.

They caught eleven trout between them. It was so peaceful that by noon they couldn’t stand it any longer, and they packed up and hurried home, looking over their shoulders the whole way.

“It’s got something in it,” Old Spout called after them. “The goose. I’m telling you.”

“Wind’s getting up,” said the fisherman who kept score.

The frogs had noticed too.

Every evening, for as long as the creek could remember, they had sung at dusk. It wasn’t music. It was a warning: *goose on patrol, goose on patrol.*

For a week there had been nothing to warn anybody about. So the frogs sat in the reeds, silent, and did not trust it.

Up at the farm, Farmer Grady stood at his fence looking at three days of bread.

The bread was a toll.

Ever since the night in Halloween Town when the goose had looked him in the eye and said “LATER,” it had turned up at his fence every sunrise to collect.

Grady paid in bread. He always threw it badly, a little too far or not quite far enough, because the goose seemed to enjoy having something to complain about.

The goose would eat the bread. Then, for the matter of the creek, it would march over and tear a piece out of his trouser leg.

Grady owned eleven pairs of trousers. None of them had a complete left cuff.

Now three mornings of bread lay in the grass going green, and his trousers had never looked better.

He walked down to the creek to see what was wrong. The path went past the church.

“Psst,” said the roof. “Farmer. It’s a demon. It’s stuck.”

Grady turned up his collar.

“Drafty old place,” he said.

The goose was standing on the bank. When it saw him, it stepped aside to let him pass.

“GOOD,” said the goose. “MORNING.”

Grady dropped his coffee.

“NICE,” the goose added. “TROUSERS.”

Then its stomach spoke.

“Is somebody there?” said a small, muffled, furious voice from under the feathers. “Hello? I’m STUCK.”

“Told you,” said the church.

There was only one way to send word to the other side, and Grady hated it.

He walked out to the middle of his cornfield, stuck out both arms, and stood there like a scarecrow.

It took eleven minutes.

“Farmer!” cawed Holy Crow, landing on his hat with enormous satisfaction. “On pole! HA!”

“It’s the goose.”

“Goose. What. Now.”

“It said good morning. It held the gate for me. It let a duckling go first. And its stomach is complaining, in sentences.”

Holy Crow looked toward the creek, then at Grady, then at the creek again.

“Bad,” he said. “Bring.”

Grady loaded the goose into a wheelbarrow and set off up the farm road.

“Bell tower!” Old Spout shouted as they passed. “Ask for the puller! Behind the bell!”

“PLEASANT. BREEZE,” said the goose.

Old Spout sighed, leaned over, and muttered the whole business down his drainpipe.

Gargoyles talk gutter to gutter. A word dropped into a downspout in one world comes out of a downspout in the other. It is the fastest news service ever built, and in two hundred years nobody has subscribed.

So by the time the wheelbarrow squeaked through the candy corn gate, the three gargoyles on the Halloween Town bell tower knew everything.

Their names were Grimsby, Lintel and Doug.

“That goose has a demon stuck in it,” Grimsby announced, before the wheel had stopped turning.

“Hush,” said the Mayor. “We’re trying to find out what’s wrong with the goose.”

The town gathered round, and wished it hadn’t.

The goose greeted the zombies. It complimented the Mayor’s posture. It told the mummy that beige was a good color on him.

“I liked it better angry,” whispered Fluffy. “I knew where I stood. Behind something.”

Griselda Wartwhistle came out of her kitchen with flour to the elbows. She had been practicing honest pie for a week. So far she had produced nine, and four were in use around town as doorstops.

She put her ear to the goose.

“Wartwhistle?” said the stomach. “Get me OUT of here. I ate every grudge this bird ever had. The egg, the bridges, Tuesdays. Now I’m too fat to fit back up the neck, and there’s nothing left in here but manners. It said ‘pardon me’ to a *rock*.”

Griselda straightened up, looking grave.

“It’s a demon,” she told the square. “It’s stuck.”

“We SAID that,” said Lintel.

“Somebody will have to pull him out. But with what?”

“We have a demon puller,” said Grimsby.

“If only there were some kind of device,” said the Mayor.

“Bell tower,” said Lintel.

“Behind the bell,” said Doug. “It’s brass.”

“Last week,” the Mayor told Grady, “they said their cousin invented the moon.”

“He did,” said Doug.

The town got to work.

Harold went first. He was a detective, and he believed in talking to suspects.

“Come out with your hands up,” he told the goose’s stomach.

“I can’t get my hands up. That’s the entire problem.”

The Mayor formed a committee. In forty minutes it got as far as choosing a name.

“Puller,” said Grimsby. “Bell tower.”

Griselda tried pie number ten, on the theory that it would bring anything back up.

It was gray. It had sunk in the middle. A fork placed in it earlier had not been recovered.

The goose took one bite.

“DELICIOUS.”

“Well, now I’m insulted,” said Griselda.

Fluffy suggested turning the goose upside down and shaking it.

Grady held the feet. Harold held Grady. They shook.

Out fell a button, two fishing lures and a hat belonging to the fisherman who kept score.

“Those are mine now,” said the stomach. “Put them back.”

“THANK. YOU. FOR. TRYING,” said the goose, upside down.

Sam tried staring at it. This works on most things. It did not work on this.

Big Bertha offered to roll over it, gently. This was put to a vote and lost.

“Behind the bell,” said Doug, to nobody. “Brass. Has a crank.”

“Has anyone ever checked?” said a small voice.

It was Squeak. It was her turn to go first.

“Ever checked?” said Pip.

The square went quiet.

Fred looked at the Mayor. The Mayor looked at Harold. Harold looked in his notebook, which was no help.

In three hundred years, nobody had once gone up to look.

“Pip. Squeak,” said Fred. “The bell tower.”

They were back in under a minute, towing something between them on a rope.

It was brass. It had a crank.

It looked like the offspring of a butter churn and a trombone, with a funnel on one side and a rubber plunger on the end of a long chain. A small plaque read: PULLER, DEMON. FOR PERSONS, BEASTS, FOWL AND SUNDRY. POINT AWAY FROM FACE.

The gargoyles said nothing. They had waited three centuries for this and they intended to enjoy it.

The instructions were engraved on the handle.

*One. Apply plunger to the afflicted. Mouth, beak, snout or muzzle. If none, improvise.*

“It’s got a beak,” said Harold, checking. “We’re in luck.”

The goose opened wide without being asked.

*Two. Crank until the demon objects.*

Grady cranked. The machine wheezed. The chain went tight.

“Ow. OW. That’s my ear. I think that’s my ear.”

“It’s objecting,” said Fluffy.

*Three. Everybody pull.*

Grady took hold of the chain. Harold took hold of Grady. The Mayor took hold of Harold, the zombies took hold of the Mayor, and the ghosts took hold of the zombies as well as ghosts can.

Big Bertha anchored the line. Sam sat on Bertha and supervised.

Pip and Squeak each grabbed a link and flapped as hard as they could, which helped nobody and made them feel wonderful.

“On three,” called Fred. “One. Two—”

“WAIT,” said the stomach. “I’ve changed my mind, it’s cozy, I’ll pay RENT—”

“Three!”

The goose’s neck stretched. The chain hummed like a fiddle string. Something inside went *thup, thup, thup*, like a boot coming out of mud.

**POP.**

Grudge came out of the beak like a cork out of a bottle, round as a dumpling. He sailed the length of the square, bounced off the bell, and dropped into the puller’s funnel with a satisfied *glunk*.

A glass jar slid out of the bottom, sealed, with a demon in it.

The whole tug-of-war fell over backward into a heap.

“Sorry,” said a ghost, from somewhere inside a zombie.

The goose shut its beak. It blinked.

It looked at Farmer Grady, flat on his back with his trousers, for once, entirely intact.

It climbed down, walked over, selected the left cuff with great care, and tore off a piece.

“There you are!” cried Grady.

“BREAD,” said the goose. “TOO. FAR.”

The square cheered. The goose hissed at them. They cheered louder.

Then, slowly, four hundred heads turned to look up at the bell tower.

Grimsby examined his claws.

“Anything you’d like to say?”

Fred rolled forward and cleared his throat.

“On behalf of Halloween Town, I apologize. From this day on, when the gargoyles speak, we will listen.”

“Hear, hear,” said the Mayor.

“Good,” said Grimsby. “Then you should know the thirteenth table is back. It’s behind the bakery. It’s bigger, and it’s got a tablecloth on now.”

There was a pause.

The Mayor chuckled. Then Harold did. Then the whole square was laughing.

“The table,” said the Mayor, wiping his eye. “Oh, that’s very good.”

Everybody went to bed.

That left the jar.

Griselda wouldn’t take it. Pie number eleven was rising, and she thought she finally had a crust.

So the gargoyles kept it on the highest ledge of the bell tower. After three hundred years they had an audience at last, and it could not walk away.

“It all started,” said Lintel, “with my cousin and the moon.”

Grudge put his face against the glass and hated every word.

By the third night he was shouting down at the street himself.

“THEY’RE RIGHT ABOUT THE TABLE! I CAN SEE IT FROM HERE! IT MOVED!”

Nobody listened to him either.

Back on Miller Creek, the frogs sang again at dusk: *goose on patrol, goose on patrol.*

On Saturday, three fishermen went into the hedge. They said it was the best tournament in years.

And on Sunday morning Farmer Grady threw his bread much too far, and leaned on the fence to wait.

“That post is rotten,” said the church roof.

“Wind’s up,” said Grady.

The post gave way.

After all, not every little pumpkin would stop a whole town to ask whether anyone had ever checked.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And nobody, in either world, listens to the gargoyles. 🎃🪿
