# Have You Got a Minute?

The gargoyles saw them first.

It was a Tuesday evening, foggy, quiet, the kind of night when Halloween Town did its laundry. Up on the bell tower, Doug leaned out and squinted toward the candy corn gate.

A white van was parked in the woods outside it. Painted on the side, in cheerful blue letters, were the words *DOORSTEP DYNAMICS — WE'LL BE RIGHT WITH YOU.*

"Salesmen," said Doug.

"Salesmen," agreed Lintel, darkly.

"Six of them," said Grimsby. "And they've got a clipboard."

"Everybody hide!" shouted all three gargoyles at once.

Down in the square, the Mayor looked up at the tower and went on sweeping the fountain.

Nobody listened.

The van door slid open.

Out stepped six people in matching blue polo shirts, smiling the kind of smile that has been practiced in a mirror.

There was Chip, who sold vacuums. There was Brenda, who sold siding, gutters and solar panels, sometimes all on one roof. There was Mr. Pruitt, gray and soft-spoken, who sold life insurance. There was Dale, who sold timeshares and had a tan in October.

And there were Gary and Larry, identical twins, who sold extended warranties for anything at all and finished each other's sentences.

They had followed a crooked sign through the woods. Dale had seen it first.

"Untapped market," he'd whispered. "Nobody's ever knocked on these doors."

He was right. Nobody had ever knocked on the doors of Halloween Town.

Nobody had ever needed to.

Chip went first.

He chose the most haunted house on Main Street, walked up the creaking steps, and rang the bell. The bell screamed. Chip didn't blink.

A ghost floated through the closed door to see who it was.

"Good evening!" said Chip. "I see you've got a bit of a dust problem."

"I'm a ghost," said the ghost.

"And that's nothing to be ashamed of. Do you have three minutes for a free demonstration?"

Before the ghost could answer, Chip had a vacuum out of its box and running.

It was the Hoover-Hurricane 9000. It had eleven attachments and a lifetime guarantee. With a mighty whoosh, it sucked up forty years of cobwebs, a rug, two candlesticks and most of the ghost.

"Ah," said Chip, peering into the canister. "Now that's what we call deep cleaning."

"Let me OUT," said the canister.

"I'll put you down for the deluxe model," said Chip.

Brenda went next door to the vampire's house.

The vampire answered the door in his cape, with his fangs out and his eyes red. He had been terrifying villagers for three hundred years.

Brenda looked up at his roof.

"Sir," she said, "have you ever considered solar?"

"I'm a VAMPIRE."

"Then think of the savings! You're never up during the day. All that sunshine going to waste."

The vampire hissed at her.

"That's a draft," said Brenda. "Your siding's letting in a terrible draft. I can hear it from here."

Mr. Pruitt visited the mummy.

He sat on the mummy's sofa with his briefcase on his knees and spoke very gently, as he always did.

"Have you thought about what would happen to your family if you were no longer here?"

"I've been dead for four thousand years," said the mummy.

Mr. Pruitt's eyes lit up.

"Then you're very low risk."

Halloween Town fought back.

The werewolf leapt out of an alley in front of Dale and howled at the moon, long and horrible and loud.

Dale nodded sympathetically. "That cough sounds terrible, buddy. You know what's great for that? Warm, dry air. Like Florida. I've got a two-bedroom condo, Fort Lauderdale, one week a year, every year, forever."

The werewolf stopped howling.

"Forever?" he said.

"Forever," said Dale, and handed him a pen.

The skeletons tried rattling their bones at Gary and Larry.

"Those joints are making a—" said Gary.

"—clicking noise," said Larry.

"You'll want to cover that with—"

"—our five-year extended warranty."

"Parts and—"

"—labor."

The skeletons, who had no parts and had never labored in their lives, looked at each other in a panic and signed.

Up on the fountain, Pip and Squeak watched this with growing horror.

"They're echoing," whispered Pip.

"They're echoing!" whispered Squeak.

"They're better at it than we are."

Squeak puffed up to twice her size. "Nobody," she said, "echoes better than us."

By midnight it was a catastrophe.

The salesmen held a FREE INFORMATIONAL SEMINAR in the town square with FREE LUNCH, and four hundred citizens of Halloween Town attended, because nobody in Halloween Town had ever refused anything free.

The lunch was one cold sandwich each.

The seminar lasted four hours.

When the lights came up, the Mayor had bought an extended warranty for the fountain. Griselda had a timeshare in Fort Lauderdale she could not use, because witches are not allowed in Florida after an incident in 1692. Big Bertha had been talked into gutters.

Harold had bought vinyl siding.

"I'm made of straw," he kept saying. "Where am I going to put siding?"

"It comes in eleven colors," said Brenda.

"Eleven?" said Harold, despite himself.

And the worst of it was that they would not leave.

You could close the door, and they'd knock again, smiling. You could hide behind the sofa, and they would wait on the porch. You could say "no" in every language the dead had ever spoken, and Mr. Pruitt would nod gently and say, "I completely understand. Can I ask what's holding you back?"

When Fred finally rolled down from the tower to see what was going on, Dale spotted him from across the square.

"Hey there, little guy," said Dale. "Is your mom or dad home?"

"I'm the princess," said Fred.

"Fantastic. Then you're the decision-maker."

Within thirty seconds, Gary and Larry were standing on either side of him.

"That crown—" said Gary.

"—is it under warranty?"

"It's made of moonlight," said Fred.

"Moonlight is notoriously—"

"—unreliable."

Fred escaped by rolling under a bench, which is the only advantage of being a pumpkin.

They held an emergency meeting in the bell tower. It was the only door the salesmen hadn't found yet, because it was up three hundred steps and salesmen take the elevator.

"We tried scaring them," said the werewolf, clutching a brochure for Fort Lauderdale. "They're not scared of anything."

"We tried hiding," said a skeleton.

"We tried saying no," said the Mayor. "It made it worse."

"Has anyone tried the goose?" said Fluffy.

"Holy Crow went to ask," said Fred. "She's at a craft fair. She's too busy."

They sat in gloomy silence.

"You need somebody they can't sell to," said Doug.

Everybody ignored him.

"Somebody who's seen every trick," said Grimsby.

"Somebody who knows the game," said Lintel.

"Somebody who did it for forty-one years," said Doug, "and was better at it than all of them put together."

The Mayor sighed. "And who might that be?"

"Agnes Pettibone," said all three gargoyles together.

The room went quiet.

"The lady with the scooter?" said Harold. "From the heist?"

"The lady who wouldn't miss the semifinal for a serial killer?" said Griselda.

"She sold encyclopedias," said Doug. "Door to door. Miller County, 1961 to 2002. Top saleswoman in New England every year but one."

"What happened that one year?"

"She broke her hip and only sold to the hospital."

Fred looked at the gargoyles for a long moment.

"Holy Crow," he said.

The scooter came through the candy corn gate at dawn, playing four notes of a hymn.

Agnes Pettibone was wearing her good coat, her good pearls, and a pair of shoes she referred to as her "closers." Holy Crow rode in the basket like a hood ornament.

She stopped in the middle of the square and looked around at the sold-out town: the warranties, the siding samples, the werewolf trying to fold a Fort Lauderdale map.

"Amateurs," said Agnes.

The six salesmen of Doorstep Dynamics saw a new face in the square, an old woman on a mobility scooter, and their eyes lit up like a slot machine.

They converged.

"Ma'am!" said Chip. "I see you've got a lot of dust on that scooter—"

"Have you thought about solar for that basket?" said Brenda.

"At your age, have you considered what might happen to your loved ones?" said Mr. Pruitt gently.

"That scooter," said Gary.

"Is it under warranty?" said Larry.

"Picture this," said Dale. "Fort Lauderdale—"

Agnes held up one hand.

They stopped. All six of them. Nobody had ever stopped them before.

"Young man," she said to Chip, "how many attachments does that vacuum have?"

"Eleven," said Chip proudly.

"How many do you use?"

Chip opened his mouth.

"Two," said Agnes. "The hose and the long skinny one. Everybody uses two. The other nine live in a box in the closet until you die, and your children throw them away. Now. I'd like to show you something."

She reached into the scooter basket.

She took out a clipboard.

It was older than any of them. It was worn smooth at the edges. Clipped to the top was a yellowed order form that read *PETTIBONE ENCYCLOPEDIAS & FINE REFERENCE, EST. 1961.*

The salesmen took a step back.

"I sold twenty-six volumes of the encyclopedia to a man who was blind," said Agnes, "and he wrote me a thank-you note. In braille. In volume eleven."

She smiled at them.

"Have you boys got a minute?"

It took her an hour.

She sold Chip the thirteenth table. She explained that it was a rare antique, self-relocating, with free delivery, because it delivered itself.

She sold Brenda a solar panel for the Grand Pumpkin Tower. Brenda didn't realize until afterward that it was simply the moon, and that Agnes had not, technically, said it was hers.

She sold Mr. Pruitt a life insurance policy on a demon in a jar. Grudge signed it himself from the bell tower, out of spite, with Mr. Pruitt named as the beneficiary.

"He'll outlive you," Agnes told Mr. Pruitt. "But it's the thought that counts."

She sold Gary and Larry a two-year warranty on an echo.

"What does it cover?" said Gary.

"Repeating," said Agnes.

"Repeating," said Larry.

"See? It's working already."

That only left Dale.

Dale had held out. Dale was the best of them. Dale had sold a timeshare to a man on his way to the airport to leave the country.

"Nice try, Grandma," said Dale. "But you can't sell a timeshare to a timeshare guy."

"I wouldn't dream of it," said Agnes. "A timeshare is a week a year in the same place, forever."

She pointed up at the bell tower, where Doug was opening the door of a tall, dark grandfather clock.

"I'm offering you a week a year in *any* year you like."

Dale looked at the clock. He looked at Agnes. He looked at the clock again.

He was a timeshare man. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard.

"Any year?" he whispered.

"Any year," said Agnes. "One week. Starting today."

"What's the catch?"

"Doug drives."

Dale signed. Then all six salesmen piled into the Grandfather, because there was room. There was always room. The inside seemed much bigger than the outside.

"Where do you want to go?" asked Doug, cracking his knuckles over the levers.

"Somewhere with customers!" said Chip.

"Somewhere with a sunny roof!" said Brenda.

"Somewhere with houses—" said Gary.

"—and doors to knock on," said Larry.

Doug thought about it. He pulled a lever.

The clock bonged. The cuckoos screamed.

He sent them to a lovely, sunny week, ten thousand years ago, before anyone had invented a door.

They came back exactly one week later, sunburned, shaggy and very, very quiet.

They had spent seven days trying to sell things to people who lived in caves. Chip had tried to vacuum one. It had not gone well. Brenda had tried to sell a mammoth solar panels and had been sat on.

Gary and Larry were the only ones who had made a sale. They had sold a two-year warranty on a rock.

Nobody came to the door when they knocked, because there was no door. That was the whole problem. They had stood in front of cave after cave, with nothing to ring and nothing to knock, saying "Hello? Hello?" into the dark.

And the caves had said it back.

"*Hello? Hello?*"

"It was the echoes," whispered Larry, white as a sheet. "Every cave had one."

"Every cave—" whispered Gary.

He stopped. He could not bring himself to finish his brother's sentence.

They packed up the van in silence.

Pip and Squeak saw them off at the gate.

"Have a nice day," said Pip.

"Have a nice day!" squeaked Squeak, perfectly, one octave higher.

Gary and Larry flinched.

The van backed down the road, turned, and drove away at well over the speed limit.

Agnes Pettibone counted her commissions on the edge of the fountain while the town gathered around.

"Did we get our money back?" asked the Mayor.

"Every penny," said Agnes. "Plus fifteen percent. Plus the vacuum."

She handed the Hoover-Hurricane 9000 to the ghost who lived on Main Street. He had finally gotten out of the canister and was sitting on his porch looking shaken.

"Two attachments," she told him. "The hose and the long skinny one. Throw the rest away."

"Thank you," said the ghost.

"And Harold."

Harold looked up nervously. He was holding a sample of vinyl siding in "Harvest Gold."

"Keep it," said Agnes. "It suits you."

That afternoon, the gargoyles hung a sign on the candy corn gate. They had carved it themselves.

*NO SOLICITING. WE WARNED YOU.*

Nobody read it.

But nobody knocked, either.

After all, not every town can be saved by a little old lady with a clipboard.

But then again—

not every little old lady is Agnes Pettibone.

And nobody, in either world, out-sells her. 🎃📋🛵
