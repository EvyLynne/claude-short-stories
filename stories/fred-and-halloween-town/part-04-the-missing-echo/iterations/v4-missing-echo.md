# Fred and the Missing Echo

Autumn came back to Halloween Town the way it always did: all at once, and smelling of smoke.

The lanterns were lit. The fog rolled in on schedule. Two small bats flew the evening patrol, the way they had every night for as long as anyone could remember.

"All clear over the bakery," said Pip.

When the echo didn't come, Pip did a double take.

There was the moon. There was the bell tower. There was a Squeak-sized piece of sky with nothing in it.

And he called her name, becoming more and more frantic, hearing the wrong echo, his own voice coming back, until the lanterns burned low.

Some of the calls were taken by the fog and not returned.

By the time the last one guttered out, Fred was waking to the sound of the small, thin, lost voice calling across the rooftops. In his sleep fugue he couldn't quite understand the feeling that the night was empty.

Then he understood. Nobody was watching him.

The stone beside him was still warm.

Sam never left without a look that said she'd be back and he had better not touch anything.

This time there was no look. There was one paw print in the dew, and even the paw print was troubled.

Two gone in one night, and no one had seen a thing.

The town locked its shutters. The fog's seasonal advance was arrested, with increasingly rapid eddies between buildings, landmarks and edifices.

As one, then two, then three evenings went by with no more hope than before, a shadow crossing the candy corn gate brought promise of enlightenment.

It was a crow. A big one. He landed on Harold's hat as if he owned it.

Harold went stiff, which for a scarecrow is saying something.

"Holy Crow," he said.

It wasn't an exclamation. It was the bird's name.

"Scarecrow," said the crow. "Still. Ugly."

Crows fly in both skies. They always have. They carry what they see from one world to the other, and they are not gentle about it.

Holy Crow opened his beak and dropped something at Harold's posts.

It was a straw hat with a brown band. One piece of straw was still tucked in it.

"Porch!" he croaked. "Chair! Rocking! Coffee! Warm! Farmer! None!"

Nobody spoke.

"Creek! Quiet! Goose! Gone!"

"Is that bad?" asked Fluffy.

Holy Crow turned one black eye on him.

"Goose. Never. Quiet. Ever."

He flapped off toward the Whispering Woods, shouting "Bad! Bad! Bad!" over the treetops.

The shouting stopped halfway through the third one.

That made five.

Princess Fred called an emergency meeting before it could become six.

"We need a detective," he said.

Harold stepped forward in his tuxedo, holding a magnifying glass he had found in the lost and found.

"I stood in one spot and watched a field for twenty years," he said. "Nothing gets past me."

"Everything got past you," said Fluffy. "You were on a pole."

"I observed it getting past me. That's different."

Harold began with the obvious suspect.

Griselda Wartwhistle was at her drink stand, humming, with a mug that rattled.

"Aha!" cried Harold. "A witch! A mug! A hum!"

Griselda sighed and lifted the lid.

Then she stopped sighing.

Grudge was no longer the size of a candy corn. He was the size of a plum, wedged in so tight the mug had cracked.

"Well, look at YOU," Griselda breathed. "You were a crumb on Tuesday."

She beamed at him the way other people beam at babies.

"I have an alibi," said the demon. "I've been in a MUG."

"You're barely in the mug," said Fluffy.

"I've been eating well." Grudge licked his lips. "Best resentment I've had in a century. Blue-ribbon quality."

"Whose resentment?" demanded Harold.

"Wouldn't you like to know. Somebody BIG." The demon snickered. "Oh, your little friends are fine, by the way. Lovely spot. Very quiet. They've really put down roots."

"So they're alive," said Fred.

"Alive? They're a captive audience!" Grudge wheezed with laughter at his own joke. "All facing the same way! You should see their faces. That's all you CAN see."

Griselda cleared her throat. "You may be saying rather a lot, dear."

"I'm saying NOTHING. They're far too dim to work it out."

Harold wrote in his notebook: *Demon says nothing.*

Fluffy, who was used to being overlooked, remembered every word.

"And my misdeeds are scheduled for the bake sale," Griselda added quickly. "Salt in the sugar jars. I've got a chart. Kidnapping isn't even on it."

Harold checked the chart. It was very thorough.

"The witch is innocent," he announced. "Of this."

Griselda put the lid back on and patted it, the way you'd pat a pie that was rising nicely.

That left no suspects, three clues nobody understood, and one very small bat who refused to stay indoors.

At nightfall, Pip flew the patrol alone.

"Something moving near the Whispering Woods," he called. "It looks like a—"

Nothing.

Then, from far off in the dark, a tiny voice, one octave higher:

"…looks like a!"

It was Squeak's echo, and it was coming from the woods.

"Put down roots," Fluffy muttered. "Oh no."

Fred, Fluffy, Harold and the Mayor followed the sound to the edge of the trees.

The gnarled old tree stood where it always had.

It was not the way it always was.

There were faces in the bark.

Sam's face, furious. Pip's face, alarmed. Squeak's face, alarmed in exactly the same way. Holy Crow, beak open mid-"Bad!" The goose, outraged beyond words. A fisherman nobody recognized.

And Farmer Grady, scowling out of a knot.

"All you can see is their faces," Fluffy whispered. "He told us. He actually told us."

"Don't just stand there!" Grady's face shouted. "My nose itches!"

"Itches!" squeaked the bark beside him.

The Mayor leaned in to look.

A thick orange vine whipped out of the roots, wrapped around all twelve feet of him, and pulled.

Now there was a face in the bark wearing a top hat.

"I KNEW it," Harold whispered. "The tree did it. The tree is the culprit."

The tree shook its branches, very hard, in a way that clearly meant no.

It looked embarrassed. It looked like a tree that had been used.

"Harold," said Fred, "trees don't have orange vines."

He rolled closer to the roots.

There was a giant seed in the dirt. There was a scrap of blue satin.

"Blue ribbon," said Fluffy. "Somebody big."

"A pumpkin," said Fred.

The vine took him too.

Fred's face appeared in the trunk, right between Sam and the goose.

It was the only face in the tree that was smiling. He couldn't help it.

Harold ran for help and was caught by the ankle. Post. Whatever it was.

That left Fluffy.

The vines slid right past him.

They always did. Nobody ever noticed Fluffy.

He rolled around the back of the tree, slowly, because he was terrified and also not built for speed.

And there she was.

Big Bertha.

She was planted in the roots like a queen on a throne, her vines running up into the trunk, a blue ribbon pinned to her side.

In front of her lay a crumpled page: Griselda's old recipe, the one Squeak had flown off into the dark a year ago. It had landed in a pumpkin patch.

"One whole pumpkin," Bertha read aloud, pleased. "It never said which one."

"You did this?" said Fluffy.

Bertha jumped, which is a lot of pumpkin to jump.

"I WON," she boomed. "First place! Biggest in the county! And do you know what the judges talked about the whole time? A little runaway who became a princess."

Her vines tightened. The faces groaned.

"Every night since, a little voice has come up through the roots," she said. "It tells me I was robbed. It tells me they all owe me. It's the only one who ever agreed with me."

Far away, at a drink stand, a mug rattled happily.

"So I collected an audience. Now everybody looks at me. Forever."

"They're looking at the inside of a tree," said Fluffy.

"They're FACING my direction."

Fluffy looked at the ribbon. He looked at the faces.

"That voice isn't your friend," he said. "It's a demon in a mug, and it's been eating you for dinner."

Bertha's vines twitched.

"Nobody ever asks about my name, you know," Fluffy went on. "They ask once, I say I don't want to talk about it, and they never ask again."

Bertha frowned. "Why are you called Fluffy?"

It was the first time anyone had asked twice.

Fluffy took a breath.

"I grew up next to a dandelion field," he said. "They got in. All the way in. I'm not full of seeds. I'm full of fluff."

He turned so she could see the dent from the cauldron, the one that had never quite healed.

Then he rammed the tree.

The dent popped.

A great white cloud of dandelion fluff burst out of Fluffy and went straight up the trunk.

The tree's branches trembled.

Its knothole wrinkled.

"Ah," said the tree, which had never spoken before. "Ahh—"

"Bless you in advance," said Fluffy.

"CHOO!"

Out they flew.

Sam, claws first. Pip and Squeak, tumbling. Holy Crow, finishing his "Bad!" The goose, already attacking someone. Farmer Grady, scratching his nose with enormous relief. The Mayor, who landed on his hat. A fisherman, who asked where the creek was and was ignored.

And Fred, who bounced twice and rolled to a stop beside his best friend.

Bertha's vines went limp.

She sat alone in the roots with her ribbon, looking smaller than she ever had.

Sam stalked toward her. The goose was ahead of Sam.

"Wait," said Fred.

He rolled up to Bertha.

"You were the biggest pumpkin I ever saw," he said. "I used to look up at you every day. I thought you knew."

Bertha said nothing for a while.

"Nobody told me," she said.

Farmer Grady cleared his throat.

"That's on me," he said. "I only ever measured you, too."

Something went out of Bertha then, like a held breath.

Back at the drink stand, there was a long, thin hiss, like a balloon with a slow leak.

Griselda lifted the lid. Grudge was the size of a candy corn again, and furious about it.

"I TOLD you that you were saying too much," said the witch.

"Drat," said the demon.

Fred's sentence was swift.

Big Bertha would lead the Moonlight Parade as its first-ever float, where the whole town could look at her on purpose.

She would also apologize to the tree.

The tree accepted, and sneezed once more for good measure.

Harold closed his notebook.

"Case solved," he said. "I suspected the pumpkin all along."

"You accused a tree," said Fluffy.

"I was building suspense."

Holy Crow landed on Harold's hat one more time.

"Still! Bad! At job!"

"I'm a detective now," said Harold.

"Like. I. Said."

That night, Fred sat atop the Grand Pumpkin Tower with Sam on one side and Fluffy on the other, a little flatter than before and stuffed with a borrowed pillow.

Pip and Squeak circled overhead.

"All clear," said Pip.

"All clear!" squeaked Squeak.

Pip had never been so glad to be repeated.

Down at the drink stand, Griselda took out her chart and added a new line at the bottom.

She underlined it twice.

After all, not every little pumpkin gets pulled into a tree and comes out with every friend he went in with.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And not every best friend is Fluffy. 🎃🌳
