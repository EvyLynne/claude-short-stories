# Pumpkins in the Wild

The film crew arrived on a Monday, in a muddy green truck, with a great deal of equipment.

There were three of them. There was Penny, the director, who had a clipboard and a headset and had not slept since 2019. There was Moss, the cameraman, who had once spent eleven weeks in a hole in Antarctica waiting for a penguin to sneeze. And there was Sir Ambrose Thistlewick.

Sir Ambrose was the most famous nature narrator in the world.

He had narrated whales. He had narrated volcanoes. He had once narrated a single snail crossing a garden path, and people wept in cinemas from Boston to Brisbane.

He spoke very softly, all the time, even when ordering lunch.

"We have come," whispered Sir Ambrose, as they stepped out of the truck at the edge of the Whispering Woods, "to find the rarest creature in all of New England."

"Which one?" asked Moss.

Sir Ambrose held up a newspaper clipping from the Miller County Gazette. It had a blurry photo of a small orange shape rolling past a bridge.

The headline read: LOCAL PUMPKIN SEEN ROLLING. FISHERMEN SAY "IT'S HAPPENED BEFORE."

"The wild pumpkin," whispered Sir Ambrose. "Nobody has ever filmed one in its natural habitat."

They followed a crooked sign through the woods. They walked through a gate made of candy corn.

Penny stopped.

Moss stopped.

Sir Ambrose did not stop. Sir Ambrose had seen everything, so he simply lowered his voice even further.

"Extraordinary," he breathed. "A whole ecosystem."

Halloween Town knew they were coming before they reached the gate. The gargoyles had seen the truck. They had shouted a warning from the bell tower.

Nobody listened, so the crew got all the way to the town square before anyone noticed them.

Fred was the first.

He was sitting on top of the Grand Pumpkin Tower in his crown, minding his own business, when he saw a man in a khaki vest kneeling in the bushes, pointing a camera the size of a cannon straight at him.

Next to the camera, an older man with white hair was whispering into a microphone.

"And here," whispered Sir Ambrose, "high on his rocky perch, we find him. The wild pumpkin. A young male, I'd guess. Notice the leaf. Notice the crown, which he has almost certainly stolen from a smaller animal."

"I didn't steal it," said Fred. "I was crowned."

Sir Ambrose did not hear him. Nature narrators never hear the animals.

"He is making a warning call," whispered Sir Ambrose. "Remarkable."

Fred decided that the best thing to do was act natural.

The trouble was, Fred had no idea how a wild pumpkin was supposed to act. He had never been wild. He had been in a pumpkin patch, then in a crown.

So he sat very still and tried to look like a pumpkin.

He sat still for an hour.

"He is playing dead," whispered Sir Ambrose. "A classic defense. The predator loses interest and moves on."

The predator did not lose interest. Moss had once waited eleven weeks for a penguin. He could wait out a pumpkin.

At hour two, Fred's leaf itched. He wiggled it.

"MOVEMENT," hissed Penny into her headset. "We have MOVEMENT."

"Did you get it?" whispered Sir Ambrose.

"I got it," whispered Moss, with tears in his eyes.

The rest of Halloween Town found out about the cameras by lunchtime, and that was the end of anybody acting natural.

The vampire came out in his best cape and posed dramatically on his balcony in the full sun, which he regretted. The zombies took turns shuffling past the camera, then running back around the block to shuffle past again. Big Bertha parked herself directly in front of the lens and refused to move.

"Ah," whispered Sir Ambrose. "The giant pumpkin. The great matriarch of the herd. She is guarding the young."

"I am getting my good side," said Bertha.

Harold was the worst.

Harold had been a scarecrow for twenty years. He had never been looked at by anything except crows, and he was not going to waste his chance.

He stood in the middle of the square with his arms out, in his tuxedo, in a heroic pose, for the entire afternoon.

"And here," whispered Sir Ambrose, "a scarecrow. Introduced to the area by farmers. Entirely harmless."

Harold was offended for a week.

Pip and Squeak were a hit.

They flew loops around the bell tower for the camera, then dove, then flew loops again.

"The bats," whispered Sir Ambrose, "are communicating by echolocation. They send out a call, and it bounces back."

"Hello!" called Pip.

"Hello!" called Squeak.

"The echo," whispered Sir Ambrose, moved. "Perfect. Nature is perfect."

"I'm not an echo," said Squeak. "I'm a whole bat."

"A whole bat!" said Pip, before he could stop himself.

Squeak looked at him.

"It's catching," said Pip.

Then Moss turned the camera toward the town's most elusive resident.

Sam.

Sam did not want to be filmed.

Every time Moss pointed the camera at her, she walked out of the shot. Not quickly. Not in a panic. She simply stood up, turned around, and left, at exactly the same pace every time.

Moss followed her to the bakery. She left.

Moss followed her up the bell tower. She left.

Moss set up a hidden camera in a hollow log by the fountain with a saucer of cream in front of it, and lay in the bushes for six hours without moving.

The next morning, the saucer was empty.

The log was empty.

There was a single paw print in the cream, on the lens.

"The black cat," whispered Sir Ambrose, the next morning, very shaken. "In fifty years of nature films, I have filmed the snow leopard. I have filmed the giant squid. I have never seen anything like her. She is a ghost."

"There's an actual ghost right behind you," said Fluffy, helpfully.

A ghost waved.

Sir Ambrose did not turn around. He had eyes only for the cat.

On the third day, the goose came.

Farmer Grady had heard there was a film crew in Halloween Town, and he wanted to see it. The goose came because the goose went everywhere with him now.

It walked into the square. It saw three strangers crouched in its bushes. It saw a camera the size of a cannon.

"WHO," said the goose.

"Ah," whispered Sir Ambrose, delighted. "The Canada goose. Common, but always a pleasure. Notice the gentle—"

"YOU," said the goose.

It came across the square like a sack of wet laundry thrown from a great height.

Moss kept filming. He had been filming a long time. He knew that you never, ever stop filming.

He filmed the goose coming at him. He filmed the goose biting his khaki vest. He filmed Penny running into the bakery. He filmed Sir Ambrose being chased twice around the fountain at speed, while still whispering.

"The goose," whispered Sir Ambrose, sprinting, "is highly territorial—"

"MINE," said the goose.

"—and should not be approached—"

"MINE."

"—under any circumstances—"

They went up a tree.

The crew stayed in the tree until sundown, when Farmer Grady came and fetched the goose with a piece of bread.

They climbed down. They packed up the truck. They had filmed for three days, and they were very tired.

But before they left, they wanted to film one last thing.

"The sunset," whispered Sir Ambrose. "Over Halloween Town. The perfect ending."

Moss set up the camera on the hill by the candy corn gate, pointing at the town. The sky turned orange and purple. The lanterns came on, one by one.

Sir Ambrose cleared his throat for the last words of the film.

He did not get to say them.

Because the whole town had come to the hill behind them, very quietly, to film them back.

Penny turned around first.

Four hundred citizens of Halloween Town were watching from the bushes. The zombies had borrowed binoculars. The vampire had a notebook. The Mayor was holding Agnes Pettibone's old video camera, which she had lent him, and which still had a recording of her 1987 Christmas on it.

And in front of them all, on a branch, was Pip, with Squeak right behind him, and both of them were whispering.

"And here," whispered Pip, in his very best Sir Ambrose voice, "we find them at last. The rare documentary crew."

"The rare documentary crew!" whispered Squeak.

"Notice the vest," whispered Pip. "Notice the enormous camera. They have come a long way from their natural habitat."

"Their natural habitat!"

"They are making a warning call," whispered Pip.

Sir Ambrose opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"Remarkable," whispered every citizen of Halloween Town at once.

The crew left that night, very quietly.

The documentary came out the following spring. It was called *Pumpkins in the Wild*, and it won three awards.

Fred watched it on channel nine at Agnes Pettibone's house, if you jiggle the antenna.

It had an hour of him sitting still. It had Bertha's good side. It had Harold described as harmless, which he wrote a letter about. It had the goose chase, which became the most famous ninety seconds in the history of nature films.

The very last shot of the film was the sunset over Halloween Town.

And right at the end, for one second, a black cat walked across the middle of the frame, stopped, turned her head, and looked straight into the camera.

Then she walked out of the shot.

"Did she do that on purpose?" asked Fluffy.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

After all, not every little pumpkin gets to be the star of a nature documentary.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And not every wild animal is the one being watched. 🎃🎥🌿
