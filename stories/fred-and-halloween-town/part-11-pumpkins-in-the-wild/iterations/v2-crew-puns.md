# Pumpkins in the Wild

The film crew arrived on a Monday in a muddy green truck.

There was Dee Rector, the director, who had a clipboard and had not slept since 2019. There was Lenny Lens, the cameraman, who had once spent eleven weeks in a hole in Antarctica waiting for a penguin to sneeze. There was Mike Boom, who held a microphone on a long pole over everything, including lunch.

And there was Sir Ambrose Thistlewick, the most famous nature narrator in the world.

Sir Ambrose had narrated whales and volcanoes. He had once narrated a snail crossing a garden path, and people wept in cinemas from Boston to Brisbane. He whispered everything, even his lunch order.

He held up a clipping from the Miller County Gazette: LOCAL PUMPKIN SEEN ROLLING. FISHERMEN SAY "IT'S HAPPENED BEFORE."

"The wild pumpkin," he whispered. "Nobody has ever filmed one."

They followed a crooked sign through the woods and walked through a gate made of candy corn.

Up on the bell tower, the gargoyles shouted a warning. Nobody listened.

Fred was sitting on top of the Grand Pumpkin Tower when he noticed a camera the size of a cannon pointed straight at him.

"And here," whispered Sir Ambrose, "high on his rocky perch, the wild pumpkin. Notice the crown, which he has almost certainly stolen from a smaller animal."

"I didn't steal it," said Fred. "I was crowned."

Nature narrators never hear the animals.

Fred decided to act natural. He had no idea how a wild pumpkin acted, so he sat perfectly still.

He sat still for two hours.

"He is playing dead," whispered Sir Ambrose. "A classic defense."

At the end of the second hour, Fred's leaf itched. He wiggled it.

"MOVEMENT!" hissed Dee.

"I got it," whispered Lenny, with tears in his eyes.

By lunch, all of Halloween Town knew about the camera, and nobody acted natural again.

The vampire posed on his balcony in his best cape and immediately regretted it, because it was noon. He began to smoke gently around the edges. He finished his close-up under a beach umbrella the zombies lent him.

The zombies shuffled past the camera, then ran around the block to shuffle past again.

Big Bertha parked directly in front of the lens.

"The great matriarch," whispered Sir Ambrose, "guarding her young."

"I'm getting my good side," said Bertha.

Harold stood in the square in his tuxedo, arms out, in a heroic pose, all afternoon.

"A scarecrow," whispered Sir Ambrose. "Introduced by farmers. Entirely harmless."

Harold was offended for a week.

Pip and Squeak did loops around the bell tower.

"The bats are echolocating," whispered Sir Ambrose. "They send out a call, and it bounces back."

"Hello!" called Pip.

"Hello!" called Squeak.

"Nature is perfect," whispered Sir Ambrose.

"I'm not an echo," said Squeak. "I'm a whole bat."

"A whole bat!" said Pip, before he could stop himself.

Then Lenny tried to film Sam.

Every time he pointed the camera at her, she stood up and walked out of the shot. She didn't hurry. She just left.

Lenny hid a camera in a log with a saucer of cream in front of it and lay in the bushes all night.

In the morning, the cream was gone. On the lens was a single paw print.

"I have filmed the snow leopard," whispered Sir Ambrose, shaken. "I have never seen anything like her. She is a ghost."

"There's an actual ghost behind you," said Fluffy.

The ghost waved. Sir Ambrose didn't turn around.

On the third day, the goose arrived with Farmer Grady.

It saw three strangers in its bushes.

"WHO," said the goose.

"Ah, the Canada goose," whispered Sir Ambrose. "Notice the gentle—"

"YOU," said the goose.

Lenny kept filming. You never stop filming.

He filmed the goose biting Mike Boom's microphone. He filmed Dee running into the bakery. He filmed Sir Ambrose being chased twice around the fountain, still whispering.

"Highly territorial—"

"MINE."

"—do not approach—"

"MINE."

They spent the rest of the day up a tree.

At sunset, the crew set up on the hill for the final shot of the film.

Sir Ambrose cleared his throat to speak the closing words.

He turned around instead.

Four hundred citizens of Halloween Town were in the bushes behind him, filming him back. The zombies had binoculars. The Mayor had Agnes Pettibone's old video camera, which still had her 1987 Christmas on it.

On a branch in front of them, Pip and Squeak were whispering.

"And here," whispered Pip, "the rare documentary crew."

"The rare documentary crew!" whispered Squeak.

"Far from their natural habitat," whispered Pip. "Notice the enormous camera. They are making a warning call."

Sir Ambrose opened his mouth, then closed it.

"Remarkable," whispered the whole town.

The crew left that night, very quietly.

*Pumpkins in the Wild* won three awards. Fred watched it on channel nine at Agnes Pettibone's house, if you jiggle the antenna.

It had two hours of him sitting still, Bertha's good side, and the goose chase, which became the most famous ninety seconds in nature film history.

The very last shot was the sunset over Halloween Town. For one second, a black cat walked into the middle of the frame, turned her head, and looked straight into the camera.

Then she walked out of the shot.

"Did she do that on purpose?" asked Fluffy.

Nobody needed to answer.

After all, not every little pumpkin gets to star in a nature documentary.

But then again—

not every little pumpkin is Fred.

And not every wild animal is the one being watched. 🎃🎥🌿
